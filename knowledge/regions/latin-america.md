# Latin America — B2B Prospecting Regional Guide

## 1. Market Overview

Latin America (LATAM) is a **$5.5T combined economy** spanning 33 countries, ~650M people, and an estimated 20M+ formally registered businesses, with a far larger informal sector that varies by country. The region is not a single market — it fragments across two dominant languages (Portuguese in Brazil, Spanish in the rest), five major currencies (BRL, MXN, ARS, COP, CLP), and dramatically different regulatory regimes that share a common Civil Law heritage. Brazil alone accounts for ~40% of regional GDP, Mexico another ~25%, and the rest is split among Argentina, Colombia, Chile, Peru, and smaller economies. Understanding the region requires country-by-country execution rather than a single regional playbook, and LeadReach agents must treat each market as its own jurisdiction with distinct registries, regulators, and cultural norms.

Prospecting infrastructure is shallower than the US or EU but maturing rapidly. Government business registries are publicly queryable in all five major markets (CNPJ in Brazil, RFC in Mexico, CUIT in Argentina, RUT in Colombia and Chile), though data depth and API access vary widely. A thriving commercial data ecosystem — ReceitaWS, Econodata, Empresite, Datacenfo, BNAmericas — fills gaps that ZoomInfo and Apollo historically under-serve. LinkedIn penetration is high among white-collar professionals in major cities (São Paulo, Mexico City, Buenos Aires, Bogotá, Santiago), and WhatsApp is the dominant B2B messaging channel across the entire region, often preferred over email for follow-up after an initial contact.

**Key facts**:
- **Combined GDP**: ~$5.5T (2024), with Brazil (~$2.1T) and Mexico (~$1.8T) representing the bulk
- **Population**: ~650M across 33 countries; Brazil (215M), Mexico (130M), Argentina (46M), Colombia (52M), Chile (19M), Peru (34M)
- **Currencies**: BRL (Brazil), MXN (Mexico), ARS (Argentina — hyperinflationary), COP (Colombia), CLP (Chile), PEN (Peru)
- **Languages**: Portuguese (Brazil), Spanish (rest of region); English fluency is limited outside C-suite and tech sectors
- **Time zones**: UTC-3 (Brazil east), UTC-4 (Chile, Colombia, Peru, eastern US-equiv), UTC-5 (Mexico City), UTC-6 (Mexico Pacific)
- **Internet penetration**: ~70-80% across major markets; smartphone-first, mobile-commerce dominant
- **Business centers**: São Paulo, Mexico City (CDMX), Buenos Aires, Bogotá, Santiago, Monterrey, Campinas, Medellín, Lima

## 2. Regulatory & Compliance

LATAM data protection has converged on GDPR-inspired frameworks, but enforcement maturity varies. Brazil's LGPD is the most rigorously enforced (modeled closely on GDPR with an active regulator, the ANPD). Mexico's LFPDPPP is older (2010) and enforced intermittently by INAI, which faced budget/political turbulence in 2024-2025. Argentina, Colombia, and Chile each have their own statutes with smaller enforcement footprints but rising activity. B2B cold outreach is generally permitted under "legitimate interest" or equivalent bases, but consent requirements for marketing email/SMS are tightening and several countries require explicit opt-in for sensitive data categories.

### Brazil — LGPD (Lei Geral de Proteção de Dados, Lei 13.709/2018)
- **Regulator**: ANPD (Autoridade Nacional de Proteção de Dados)
- **Lawful bases**: Consent, contract, legal obligation, legitimate interest, public function, credit protection — for B2B outreach, **legitimate interest** is the standard basis but requires a documented Legitimate Interest Assessment (LIA)
- **Data subject rights**: Access, correction, deletion, portability, information on sharing — similar to GDPR
- **DPO requirement**: Required for high-risk processing; many mid-sized companies appoint one
- **Penalties**: Up to 2% of Brazilian revenue per violation, capped at R$50M (~$10M USD) per infraction; ANPD has issued multimillion-real fines since 2023
- **ANPD cross-border**: International data transfers require adequacy decision, SCCs, or specific consent

### Mexico — LFPDPPP (Ley Federal de Protección de Datos Personales en Posesión de los Particulares)
- **Regulator**: INAI (Instituto Nacional de Transparencia, Acceso a la Información y Protección de Datos Personales) — note INAI faced temporary shutdown risk in 2024-2025 due to political disputes, but the underlying law remains in force
- **Principles**: Lawfulness, consent, notice, quality, purpose limitation, proportionality, accountability
- **ARCO rights**: Access, Rectification, Cancellation, Opposition (similar to GDPR)
- **Consent**: Explicit for sensitive data; implicit (with notice) for non-sensitive
- **Penalties**: Up to ~$150K MXN equivalent per violation, multiplied for severe cases
- **Pending reform**: A new federal data protection bill (2024-2025) is expected to align more closely with GDPR; monitor for changes

### Argentina — PDPA (Ley 25.326 de Protección de Datos Personales)
- **Regulator**: AAIP (Agencia de Acceso a la Información Pública)
- **Regime**: Consent-based; declared "adequate" by the EU (one of only two LATAM countries with EU adequacy alongside Uruguay)
- **Reform (2024-2025)**: A modernized PDPA bill is in late-stage legislative process, bringing Argentina closer to GDPR — expands rights, breach notification, DPO requirements
- **Penalties**: Fines up to ARS 100M (recently updated for inflation), publication of sanctions, suspension of databases

### Colombia — Ley 1581 de 2012
- **Regulator**: SIC (Superintendencia de Industria y Comercio)
- **Lawful bases**: Consent, contract, legal/commercial relationship, legitimate interest (recognized via decrees)
- **HABEAS DATA**: Constitutional right of data access/correction (art. 15 of the Constitution)
- **Penalties**: Up to 2,000 current legal minimum monthly wages per violation; SIC actively fines companies for spam and unauthorized data sharing

### Chile — Law 19.628 (Ley sobre Protección de la Vida Privada)
- **Regulator**: Undergoing reform; currently scattered across SAG, Consejo CPC. A new comprehensive data protection law (2024 reform) is being finalized to align with GDPR
- **Current regime**: Older and less strict than LGPD; consent-driven but enforcement historically light
- **Reform in flight**: Expected to introduce DPO, breach notification, expanded rights, and a dedicated data protection agency (Agencia de Protección de Datos)

### Cross-Cutting Considerations
- **Anti-bribery**: Brazil Lei 12.846 (Clean Company Act), Mexico LGRA (Ley General de Responsabilidades Administrativas), and U.S. FCPA apply extraterritorially to U.S.-listed LATAM companies — relevant when prospecting state-owned enterprises (Petrobras, Pemex, Codelco, Ecopetrol)
- **Currency controls**: Argentina's cepo and multiple exchange rates (oficial, MEP, blue) complicate cross-border contracts; Colombia and Brazil have fewer controls but BRL/COP volatility is significant
- **Sanctions**: Venezuela is heavily sanctioned (OFAC); prospecting into Venezuela requires sanctions screening even from LATAM bases

## 3. Business Registries

### Brazil — Receita Federal (CNPJ) & Junta Comercial
- **CNPJ (Cadastro Nacional da Pessoa Jurídica)**: 14-digit federal tax ID issued by Receita Federal; every legal entity must have one
- **Receita Federal CNPJ query**: `solucoes.receita.fazenda.gov.br/Servicos/cnpjreva/cnpjreva_solicitacao.asp` — free, returns company name, trade name, address, CNAE (industry code), partners, status
- **ReceitaWS** (`receitaws.com.br`): Free/paid API wrapper around CNPJ queries — commonly used by LeadReach `scout` for bulk lookups; returns JSON with full company record
- **Junta Comercial**: State-level commercial registry (e.g., JUCESP for São Paulo) — holds articles of incorporation, bylaws, share capital, board composition; deeper than CNPJ but often paid
- **CNAE codes**: Brazil's industry classification (similar to NAICS) — primary and secondary codes per company
- **Simples Nacional**: Simplified tax regime for small businesses; companies on Simples have different revenue thresholds and compliance burdens

### Mexico — SAT (RFC) & IMSS
- **RFC (Registro Federal de Contribuyentes)**: 12-13 character tax ID issued by SAT (Servicio de Administración Tributaria); 12 chars for companies (e.g., `ABC010203AB1`), 13 for individuals
- **SAT RFC lookup**: `portalcli.sat.gob.mx` — search by RFC or company name; returns tax regime, address, status (activo/cancelado)
- **IMSS (Instituto Mexicano del Seguro Social)**: Employer registry — useful for headcount estimates (number of insured employees)
- **Registro Público de Comercio**: State-level commercial registry; articles of incorporation, board
- **SHCP (Secretaría de Hacienda)**: Treasury; beneficial ownership filings

### Argentina — AFIP (CUIT) & IGJ
- **CUIT/CUIL/CDI**: 11-digit tax ID (Clave Única de Identificación Tributaria) issued by AFIP; CUIT for companies, CUIL for employees, CDI for non-residents
- **AFIP Constancia de Inscripción**: `afip.gob.ar` — search by CUIT; returns tax regime, address, actividades económicas, status
- **IGJ (Inspección General de Justicia)**: National corporate registry for entities incorporated in Buenos Aires City; holds articles, board, share capital. Other provinces have their own registries (e.g., IGJ for CABA, Registro Público for provinces)
- **Padrón de Alcance 5 / 13**: AFIP reduced-data lookups used by third parties

### Colombia — Cámara de Comercio & RUT
- **NIT (Número de Identificación Tributaria)**: Issued by DIAN (Dirección de Impuestos y Aduanas Nacionales); 9-digit base + verification digit
- **RUT (Registro Único Tributario)**: Master tax registration; CIIU (industrial classification) codes, tax responsibilities
- **Cámara de Comercio**: Each city (Bogotá, Medellín, Cali, Barranquilla) has its own Cámara; holds articles, board, share capital, registration renewal status — searchable via `ejemplo.rues.org.co` (RUES — Registro Único de Entidades Mercantiles)
- **Lookups**: `consultaruc.com` and SIC's consumer databases

### Chile — SII (RUT) & Registro de Comercio
- **RUT (Rol Único Tributario)**: 8-9 digit ID for companies and individuals, issued by SII (Servicio de Impuestos Internos); same number is the RUN (national ID) for individuals
- **SII Consulta de RUT**: `zeus.sii.cl/cvc_cgi/stc/getbult` — returns business name, activities, tax regime, address
- **Registro de Comercio**: Held by Conservador de Bienes Raíces e Indice Comercio per commune; articles of incorporation, board modifications
- **Consejo CPC**: Audited financial statements of public-interest entities

### São Paulo
São Paulo is the financial and commercial capital of Latin America, generating roughly 30% of Brazil's GDP on its own. The city and its metro region host the headquarters of every major Brazilian bank (Itaú, Bradesco, Santander Brasil), the country's largest stock exchange (B3), and a deep advertising/marketing ecosystem anchored on agencies like WPP Brazil and Grupo ABC. The Faria Lima and Vila Olímpia corridors are dense with FinTech and SaaS startups — Nubank, Stone, Loft, QuintoAndar — making it the primary ICP target for software, financial services, and B2B SaaS sales into Brazil. LeadReach scouts targeting Brazil should default to São Paulo unless the ICP explicitly calls for another region.

### Mexico City (CDMX)
Mexico City is the political, media, and corporate headquarters capital of Mexico. Telco giant América Móvil (Carlos Slim's group), broadcaster Televisa, and major banks (Banorte, BBVA Mexico, Santander Mexico) anchor the city. Polanco and Santa Fe are the densest business districts; government-adjacent prospects (Pemex, CFE, SHCP) cluster in central CDMX. Monterrey handles heavy industry; Guadalajara handles tech (the "Mexican Silicon Valley" with Intel, Oracle, and Tata operations); but CDMX remains the executive decision-maker hub for cross-border outreach.

### Buenos Aires
Buenos Aires (and its microcentro / Puerto Madero corridor) is the corporate HQ hub for Argentina despite the country's macro volatility. MercadoLibre (LatAm's largest e-commerce company), Globant (NYSE-listed tech services), and the Techint group (Tenaris, Ternium) anchor the city. Agribusiness majors (Cargill Argentina, Aceitera General Deheza, Bunge Argentina) base commercial operations here. Argentine executives are highly internationalized (many US/EU MBAs), and English fluency is the highest in LATAM outside Brazil's tech sector.

### Bogotá
Bogotá is Colombia's political and financial capital, hosting Bancolombia (the country's largest bank), Grupo Aval (multi-bank holding), Ecopetrol (state oil), and Avianca (regional airline). The city's financial district (Centro Internacional, Calle 72) concentrates banking, insurance, and BPO operations. Bogotá's Zona T and Parque 93 are where tech companies cluster; Rappi (the regional delivery decacorn) splits operations between Bogotá and Medellín. Colombia's bilingual workforce is growing — many prospecting conversations can be conducted in English, but Spanish opens far more doors.

### Santiago
Santiago is Chile's economic and political capital and the HQ hub for the country's mining industry — Codelco (state copper, world's largest), Antofagasta Minerals, and the regional offices of BHP, Rio Tinto, and Anglo American. Chile's pension fund system (AFPs — Habitat, Cuprum, Modelo, Provida, Capital) manages ~$200B USD in assets and is unique in LATAM; financial services prospecting here is distinctive. Retailers Falabella, Cencosud, and Ripley run regional operations from Santiago. Wine, salmon, and forestry industries base commercial HQs in Santiago with operations in regions (Valparaíso, Bío Bío, Los Lagos).

### Monterrey
Monterrey is Mexico's industrial capital, with steel (Ternium Mexico), cement (Cemex global HQ), beverages (FEMSA, Coca-Cola bottler), and conglomerates (Alfa, Cydsa, Xignux). The city has a distinctive business culture — highly entrepreneurial, US-facing (Monterrey is closer to Texas than to CDMX in many commercial respects), and bilingual at senior levels. LeadReach scouts targeting Mexican manufacturing, logistics, or industrial SaaS should prioritize Monterrey alongside CDMX.

### Campinas
Campinas and the broader São Paulo state interior (São José dos Campos, São Carlos) form Brazil's deep-tech and aerospace corridor. Embraer (third-largest aircraft manufacturer globally) is headquartered in São José dos Campos; CPqD (telecom research) is in Campinas; Unicamp (one of Latin America's top research universities) drives biotech and quantum computing spinouts. Prospecting for advanced manufacturing, pharma R&D, or aerospace suppliers should not miss this region.

### Medellín
Medellín has rebranded from a turbulent 1990s to a 2020s innovation hub — the city's Ruta N innovation district hosts FinTech, creative industries, and BPO. Bancolombia originated here (and still maintains operational HQ alongside Bogotá). Grupo Éxito (retail) and the EPM (Empresas Públicas de Medellín) utility anchor the city. International SaaS and customer support centers have flocked to Medellín for its climate, English proficiency, and lower cost than Bogotá.

## 5. Dominant Industries

### Brazil — Agribusiness, Mining, Aerospace, Banking
Brazil is the world's largest exporter of soybeans, coffee, sugar, orange juice, and beef (JBS, BRF, Marfrig); a top-3 iron ore producer (Vale); home to Embraer (the only non-US/EU major aircraft OEM); and a banking sector dominated by Itaú, Bradesco, Santander Brasil, and Caixa Econômica Federal. Nubank (NYSE: NU) is the world's largest digital bank by customers (90M+) and exemplifies the Brazilian FinTech wave. Petrobras (state oil, NYSE: PBR) anchors energy. Software exports are growing (TOTVS, Linx, Movile, Stone), and Brazil is a major BPO/nearshore hub for U.S. companies.

### Mexico — Manufacturing/Maquiladora, Automotive, Telecom
Mexico's manufacturing economy is built on the maquiladora model — over 5,000 export-assembly plants along the U.S. border (Tijuana, Ciudad Juárez, Monterrey, Saltillo) producing vehicles, electronics, medical devices, and aerospace components. The automotive sector hosts assembly plants for GM, Ford, VW, Audi, BMW, Nissan, Toyota, and Stellantis. Telecom is dominated by América Móvil (Carlos Slim, NYSE: AMX) and AT&T Mexico; media by Televisa and TV Azteca. Cemex (cement), FEMSA (Coca-Cola bottler + OXXO convenience stores), Grupo Bimbo (world's largest baking company), and Gruma (tortillas) are global multinationals HQ'd in Mexico.

### Argentina — Agribusiness, Lithium Mining, Tech
Argentina is a global agribusiness powerhouse (soybean crushing, beef, wheat, wine) with majors like Aceitera General Deheza, Cargill Argentina, and Bodega Catena Zapata. The country sits on the "Lithium Triangle" (with Bolivia and Chile) — lithium brine projects operated by Livent (now Arcadium Lithium), Allkem, POSCO, and Ganfeng make Argentina the world's fourth-largest lithium producer. Tech exports are led by MercadoLibre (NASDAQ: MELI, LatAm's largest e-commerce/finTech), Globant (NYSE: GLOB, software services), Despegar (travel), and MercadoPago (FinTech). YPF (state energy, NYSE: YPF) anchors Vaca Muerta shale development.

### Colombia — Coffee, Oil, FinTech
Colombia is the world's third-largest coffee producer (FNC — Federación Nacional de Cafeteros, Juan Valdez brand) and a major oil producer (Ecopetrol, NYSE: EC). Bancolombia (NYSE: CIB) is one of LatAm's largest banks; Grupo Aval is the multi-bank holding. The FinTech sector — Rappi (delivery, now also a super-app), dLocal (cross-border payments, NASDAQ: DLO), Addi (BNPL), Semrush-backed Turboro — has made Colombia the #2 LatAm FinTech hub after Brazil. Coal mining (Drummond, Glencore Cerrejón) and emeralds are also significant exports.

### Chile — Copper, Forestry, Salmon, Wine
Chile is the world's largest copper producer (Codelco, BHP Escondida, Antofagasta Minerals, Anglo American Sur) — copper accounts for ~50% of Chilean exports. Forestry (Arauco, CMPC) makes Chile a top pulp/lumber exporter. Salmon farming (Multi X, AquaChile, Cermaq, Mowi Chile) makes Chile the world's second-largest salmon producer after Norway. Wine (Concha y Toro, Santa Rita, Errázuriz) is exported globally. Lithium (SQM, Albemarle) is rising fast in the Atacama brines.

## 7. Cultural Norms for B2B

### Relationship-Driven ("Personalismo")
LATAM business culture is **fundamentally relationship-driven** in a way that surprises US exporters expecting transactional efficiency. The Portuguese term "personalismo" and Spanish "personalismo" both denote a cultural expectation that business is conducted between people, not between companies; a cold email from an unknown vendor has low conversion until a personal connection (often via LinkedIn mutual, a former colleague, or an industry association introduction) is established. LeadReach `bard` should craft outreach that opens with a personal hook (shared connection, shared university, shared industry event) rather than leading with the value proposition. Expect 3-5 touchpoints before a first meeting is granted, and expect the first meeting to spend significant time on personal background ("Where are you from?", "What university?") before any business discussion.

### Hierarchy & Seniority
LATAM corporate hierarchies are flatter than Asia but steeper than the U.S. C-suite executives (CEO, CFO, CIO) are addressed by title in early interactions ("Senhor Silva", "Doctor Martínez", "Ingeniero Gómez"), and Brazilian Portuguese uses "você/o senhor/a senhora" distinctions carefully. Decision-making authority is concentrated at the top — a Director or VP may stall a deal indefinitely if the CEO/Owner has not been looped in. Family-owned businesses (the dominant model in Mexico's GRUPOS and Brazil's family conglomerates) often route decisions through the founding family, which can extend sales cycles but produces very loyal relationships once established.

### Spanish vs. Portuguese — Never Mix
Brazil is the only Portuguese-speaking country in LATAM; the rest of the region speaks Spanish (with Brazilian-influenced regional variants). Outreach in Spanish to a Brazilian prospect is a major cultural error (Brazilians are sensitive to being treated as "Spanish-speaking Latin America"); outreach in Portuguese to a Mexican or Argentine prospect signals geographic ignorance. LeadReach `judge` should detect the country from the registry (CNPJ → Portuguese; RFC/RUT → Spanish) and `bard` should compose in the correct language. When in doubt, English is acceptable at C-suite level in multinational subsidiaries butchances of response drop ~40-60% versus native-language outreach.

### WhatsApp for B2B
WhatsApp is the dominant B2B messaging channel across LATAM, used for everything from quick follow-ups to contract negotiations. Brazil, Mexico, Argentina, Colombia, and Chile all have >90% WhatsApp smartphone penetration among white-collar professionals. WhatsApp Business API (official) and WhatsApp Business (consumer app) are both used. After an initial email or LinkedIn contact, LATAM prospects often prefer to continue via WhatsApp — LeadReach `flow` should configure multi-touch sequences that include a "would you like to continue on WhatsApp?" option. WhatsApp cold outreach without prior consent violates LGPD in Brazil and is increasingly regulated; always obtain consent before adding a prospect to a WhatsApp broadcast list.

### Time & Punctuality
Brazilian business meetings often start 10-15 minutes late ("a hora brasileira"); Mexican meetings are similarly flexible. Argentine, Chilean, and Colombian meetings tend to be more punctual, closer to US norms. LeadReach `flow` scheduling should account for this — calendar invites should be confirmed the day before, and follow-up timing should be flexible. The "mañana" stereotype is largely inaccurate (LATAM professionals work hard and long hours), but rigid US-style scheduling creates friction.

### Holidays & Calendar
- **Carnival** (Brazil, February/March — varies; effectively a full week of business closure)
- **Semana Santa** (Holy Week, all LATAM — late March/April)
- **Día de la Independencia**: September 7 (Brazil), September 16 (Mexico), July 9 (Argentina), July 20 (Colombia), September 18 (Chile)
- **Día de Muertos** (Mexico, November 1-2)
- **December 15 – January 15**: Extended summer vacation in Argentina, Chile, Uruguay; significant slowdown in Brazil, Mexico, Colombia
- **January-February**: Brazilian summer vacation (executives travel)

## 8. Outreach Patterns

### Channel Priority (LATAM)
1. **LinkedIn** — primary first touch for white-collar prospects; especially strong in Brazil, Mexico, Argentina
2. **Email** — secondary, formal; expected to be longer and more relationship-oriented than US email
3. **WhatsApp** — for follow-up after initial contact; obtain consent before adding to broadcast lists
4. **Phone** — works for warm leads; cold calling is less effective than in US
5. **In-person events** — high-impact; LATAM buyers expect to meet at industry events (Febraban, Fime, Andicom, Expomin)

### Email Script Patterns (Brazilian Portuguese)
- **Subject line**: Use a question, not a value claim — "ainda usando [ferramenta]?" or "vi sua publicação sobre [tópico] no LinkedIn"
- **Opening**: Lead with personal connection (shared connection, shared university, recent company news)
- **Body**: 2-3 short paragraphs; include specific reference to prospect's industry challenge
- **Call to action**: Soft — "conversamos 15 minutos na próxima semana?" (let's chat 15 minutes next week?)
- **Signature**: Full name, title, company, mobile/WhatsApp, LinkedIn URL, company address (LGPD requires physical address in marketing emails)
- **Opt-out**: "Caso não deseje receber mais e-mails, responda com 'descadastrar'" (LGPD requires opt-out mechanism)

### Email Script Patterns (Spanish — Mexico/Argentina/Colombia/Chile)
- **Subject line**: "¿Sigue usando [herramienta]?" / "Noté su reciente [anuncio]" (tuteo for Mexico/Colombia/Chile, vos for Argentina)
- **Opening**: Personal reference, ideally a shared connection or industry event
- **Body**: 3 short paragraphs; explicit value; reference a similar client in the region
- **CTA**: "¿Tienen 15 minutos la próxima semana?" / "¿Conversamos breve la próxima semana?"
- **Formality**: Use "usted" initially, especially with C-suite and in Mexico/Colombia; switch to "tú" only if the prospect does so first

### Follow-Up Cadence
- **Day 0**: Initial email (or LinkedIn InMail)
- **Day 2-3**: LinkedIn connection request (with personalized note)
- **Day 5-7**: WhatsApp message (if consent obtained) or second email (new angle)
- **Day 10-14**: Phone call (if phone available)
- **Day 21**: Breakup email ("fechando o ciclo" in Portuguese, "cierre de seguimiento" in Spanish)
- LATAM cycles are typically 30-50% longer than US — plan for 6-8 touches over 30-45 days

### Do's
- ✅ Use the correct language (Portuguese for Brazil, Spanish for others; never mix)
- ✅ Address prospects by title in early interactions ("Senhor", "Doctor", "Ingeniero")
- ✅ Reference a personal connection or shared industry event in opening
- ✅ Offer WhatsApp as a follow-up channel after initial email
- ✅ Include physical company address in email footer (LGPD/local law requirement)
- ✅ Confirm calendar invites the day before (LATAM meetings can slip)
- ✅ Translate marketing materials; English-language emails get 40-60% lower response rates
- ✅ Use local case studies (a Mexican prospect doesn't care about your US customer)

### Don'ts
- ❌ Don't send Spanish to Brazilian prospects or Portuguese to Spanish-speaking prospects
- ❌ Don't cold-add prospects to WhatsApp broadcast lists without consent (LGPD/LFPDPPP violation)
- ❌ Don't pitch "Latin America" as a single market — Brazilians and Mexicans do not see themselves as a region
- ❌ Don't ignore Carnival (Brazil) or Semana Santa (all LATAM) in scheduling
- ❌ Don't assume English fluency outside C-suite at multinationals
- ❌ Don't reference the political situation in Argentina, Venezuela, or Cuba in opening lines
- ❌ Don't discuss currency volatility unless the prospect raises it
- ❌ Don't send attachments over 5MB — many LATAM corporate inboxes have strict limits

## 9. Common Pitfalls

### Pitfall 1: Treating LATAM as One Market
The single most common error US/EU vendors make is "Latin America" market-entry strategy that treats the region as monolithic. Brazil's regulatory regime (LGPD, ANPD, Receita Federal), language (Portuguese), currency (BRL), and business culture differ from Mexico's as much as they differ from the U.S. A pan-LATAM email blast in Spanish to Brazilians is a guaranteed failure. LeadReach `atlas` must always decompose LATAM into country-specific prospecting tracks.

### Pitfall 2: LGPD Consent Misconceptions
Brazilian LGPD is GDPR-equivalent in rigor. Many vendors assume "B2B cold email is exempt" — it is not. While legitimate interest is the lawful basis, you must (a) document a Legitimate Interest Assessment, (b) provide a clear opt-out in every email, (c) honor opt-outs immediately, (d) include your company's full physical address, and (e) respect data subject rights (access, deletion) within ANPD's timelines. Fines are real — ANPD fined multiple companies in 2023-2024.

### Pitfall 3: Currency Volatility & Pricing
Argentine ARS lost >80% of value vs. USD in 2023-2024; BRL and COP regularly move 10-20% in a quarter. Quoting USD prices to LATAM prospects can either price you out (when local currency weakens) or trigger pushback (when local currency strengthens). Best practice: quote in USD with a local-currency estimate at the time of quote, and offer quarterly true-up for annual contracts. For Argentina specifically, understand the difference between the official rate, MEP rate, and blue rate — many Argentine companies can only legally pay at the official rate, which can mean a 100%+ pricing haircut.

### Pitfall 4: Informal Economy Size
The informal economy is huge across LATAM — Brazil ~40% of GDP, Mexico ~55%, Colombia ~55%, Peru ~70%. Many prospecting targets operate partly or fully in cash, especially in retail, construction, hospitality, and agribusiness. Credit checks, financial verification, and headcount estimates may undercount reality. Cross-check CNPJ/RFC employee counts (e.g., Brazil eSocial, Mexico IMSS) and use industry association data (CNC, CONCAMIN) to triangulate.

### Pitfall 5: Import/Export Complexity
Selling software into LATAM is one thing; selling physical goods or services requiring import is another. Brazil's complex tax system (ICMS, IPI, PIS/COFINS) can add 40-60% to landed cost of imported goods; Mexico's IVA (16%) and IEPS (selective excise) add similar burdens; Argentina's DJAI (Declaración Jurada Anticipada de Importación) import permits can stall shipments for months. LeadReach prospects importing physical products need substantial in-country logistics/tax advisory — factor this into ICP qualification.

### Pitfall 6: WhatsApp Spam Without Consent
WhatsApp cold prospecting is tempting given the channel's dominance, but bulk WhatsApp broadcasting without consent violates LGPD (Brazil), LFPDPPP (Mexico), and is increasingly enforced. WhatsApp itself bans accounts that receive multiple user reports. Use WhatsApp for warm follow-up after explicit email/LinkedIn consent, never for cold bulk outreach.

### Pitfall 7: Political Sensitivity
LATAM political environments are volatile — Argentina elected a libertarian president in 2023, Brazil returned Lula to power in 2022, Mexico elected its first female president (Claudia Sheinbaum) in 2024. Avoid political commentary in outreach; do not assume political alignment with the prospect; do not reference Venezuela, Cuba, or regional geopolitical tensions unless the prospect raises them. A neutral, professional, business-focused tone is safest.
