# Japan — B2B Lead Intelligence & Compliance Guide

## Regional Overview

Japan is the world's fourth-largest economy (~$4.2T GDP, 2025) and the second-largest B2B technology market in the Asia-Pacific region after China. With a population of ~124M, Japan combines the world's most rapidly aging demographic (median age 49, 29% over 65) with extraordinary technological sophistication in manufacturing, robotics, and enterprise IT. The country's B2B market is enormous but famously insular: Japanese companies prefer domestic vendors, business relationships are built through extended trust-building (nemawashi), and the language barrier remains significant despite high English education rates. Japan's B2B SaaS market alone is estimated at ~$15B (2025), growing at 15–20% annually, but is dominated by domestic players (Sansan, SmartHR, freee) and heavily localized global vendors (Salesforce Japan, AWS Japan).

The Japanese business landscape is defined by its keiretsu legacy (interconnected corporate groups like Mitsubishi, Mitsui, Sumitomo), a strong SME sector (3.5M+ SMEs employing 70% of the workforce), and a government actively pushing digital transformation (DX) through initiatives like "Society 5.0" and the Digital Agency (established 2021). The COVID-19 pandemic accelerated cloud adoption, but Japan remains behind the US and Europe in SaaS penetration — many enterprises still rely on on-premise systems, fax machines, and hanko (personal seal) authentication, creating a massive greenfield opportunity for B2B vendors who can navigate the cultural and regulatory complexity.

LeadReach treats Japan as a tier-1 strategic market — high ACVs (SaaS deals in Japan are typically 20–30% above US levels for enterprise), low churn once trust is established, but exceptionally long sales cycles (6–18 months for enterprise, 3–6 months for mid-market). The key differentiator for LeadReach is culturally-adapted, compliance-aware prospecting: Scout identifies Japanese accounts with DX intent signals, Forge enriches with registry data and keiretsu affiliation, Sage researches the APPI compliance context and sector regulations, Bard composes Japanese-language outreach with appropriate formality levels, and Judge scores accounts by Japan-fit (language readiness, local entity, reference availability).

**Key facts**:
- **GDP**: ~$4.2T USD (2025); 4th largest globally
- **Population**: ~124M; declining (~0.5% annual decrease); median age 49
- **Currency**: JPY (Japanese Yen); has depreciated significantly from ~110 to ~150 per USD (2023–2025)
- **Government**: Constitutional monarchy with parliamentary democracy; bicameral Diet (House of Representatives, House of Councillors)
- **Languages**: Japanese (exclusive official language); English proficiency low relative to GDP (ranked ~80th on EPI)
- **Time zone**: JST (UTC+9); no daylight saving time
- **Internet penetration**: ~93%; mobile penetration ~95%
- **Business centers**: Tokyo (finance, SaaS, corporate HQs), Osaka (manufacturing, trading), Nagoya (automotive, industrial), Fukuoka (startups, fintech), Yokohama (IT, biotech)

## Business Registration Infrastructure

Japan's corporate registration system is centralized but paper-based in many respects, with digital modernization ongoing through the Digital Agency.

### Legal Affairs Bureau (法務局 — Hōmukyoku)

- **Commercial Register (登記簿 — Tōkibo)**: All Japanese companies must register with the Legal Affairs Bureau in their jurisdiction. The register includes company name, registered address, directors, capital, and business purposes.
- **Corporate Number (法人番号 — Hōjin Bangō)**: A 13-digit identifier assigned by the National Tax Agency to all corporations for tax purposes. This is the closest Japan has to a universal business identifier. Searchable online at `hojin.or.jp`.
- **Company types**: Kabushiki Kaisha (KK — 株式会社, stock company, most common for larger businesses); Gōdō Kaisha (GK — 合同会社, limited liability company, increasingly popular for startups); Other forms include Gōmei Kaisha (partnership), Gōshi Kaisha (limited partnership).
- **Access**: Commercial register extracts (登記簿謄本 — Tōkibo Tōhon) can be obtained from the Legal Affairs Bureau, increasingly available online. Corporate Number lookup is free at `hojin.or.jp`.

### National Tax Agency (国税庁 — Kokuzeichō)

- **Corporate Number system**: Implemented in 2015 under the Act on the Use of Numbers to Identify a Specific Individual in Administrative Procedures. All corporations receive a 13-digit Corporate Number.
- **Corporate Number publication**: Available at `hojin.or.jp` — free search by name, number, or address. Includes legal name, Corporate Number, registered address, and registration date.
- **Tax filings**: Corporate tax returns are not publicly available, but the National Tax Agency publishes aggregate statistical data.

### Financial Services Agency (FSA — 金融庁)

- **EDINET** (`disclosure2.edinet-fsa.go.jp`): Electronic Disclosure for Investors' NETwork. All public company financial filings (securities reports, annual reports, quarterly reports) are available. Essential for due diligence on listed Japanese companies.
- **Listed companies**: ~3,800 companies listed on Tokyo Stock Exchange (TSE) Prime, Standard, and Growth markets (restructured from TSE1/TSE2/Mothers/JASDAQ in 2022).

### Company Data Ecosystem

- **Teikoku Databank (TDB)** (`tdb.co.jp`): Japan's largest corporate credit research agency. Provides comprehensive company reports including financials, payment history, and credit ratings. Essential for B2B due diligence.
- **Tokyo Shōkō Research (TSR)** (`tsr-net.co.jp`): Second-largest credit research agency. Competitor to TDB with similar coverage.
- **Kigyō Jōhō (企業情報)**: Various commercial data providers aggregate corporate data from public registries and credit agencies.

## Regulatory & Compliance Framework

Japan's data protection framework is anchored by the Act on the Protection of Personal Information (APPI), which has been significantly strengthened through amendments in 2020 and 2022, bringing it closer to GDPR standards.

### APPI — Act on the Protection of Personal Information (個人情報保護法 — Kojin Jōhō Hogo Hō)

The APPI is Japan's comprehensive data protection law, administered by the Personal Information Protection Commission (PPC — 個人情報保護委員会).

- **Scope**: Applies to all business operators handling personal information in Japan. Extraterritorial application — applies to foreign businesses handling Japanese residents' personal information.
- **Definition of personal information**: Information about a living individual that can identify the individual, including name, date of birth, and other descriptions. The 2022 amendment expanded the definition to include "personal referential information" — information that can identify an individual when combined with other information (similar to GDPR's broader definition).
- **Purpose limitation**: Must specify the purpose of use when acquiring personal information and cannot use it beyond that purpose without consent.
- **Consent**: Generally, consent is required for the use and provision of personal information beyond the specified purpose. However, the APPI allows certain exceptions for business operations. Consent must be obtained in a manner that is "as appropriate as possible" — not as prescriptive as GDPR but moving toward similar standards.
- **Cross-border transfer**: The 2022 amendment significantly tightened rules for transferring personal information overseas:
  - Requires the data subject's consent **in advance** (not after the fact)
  - Must provide the data subject with information about the recipient country's data protection system
  - Must ensure the recipient implements necessary and appropriate measures
  - The recipient country must have a data protection system equivalent to Japan's (adequacy determination)
  - The EU-Japan adequacy arrangement (2019) facilitates data flows between the EU and Japan
- **Data breach notification**: The 2022 amendment introduced mandatory breach notification. Businesses must report breaches to the PPC and notify affected individuals when: (1) the breach involves sensitive personal information, (2) the breach may cause financial damage, (3) the breach involves more than 1,000 individuals, or (4) the breach was caused by fraud or other illicit means.
- **Individual rights**: Right to request disclosure, correction, cessation of use, and deletion of personal information. Must respond "without delay" (formally "without unreasonable delay" — typically interpreted as 30 days).
- **Fines**: The 2022 amendment introduced administrative fines of up to ¥100M (~$670K USD) for the most serious violations. Criminal penalties of up to ¥100M for businesses and up to 1 year imprisonment for individuals.
- **PPC enforcement**: The PPC has been increasingly active in enforcement, issuing compliance orders and fines. In 2023, the PPC issued its first administrative fine under the amended APPI.

### B2B Marketing Specifics

- **Cold email**: The APPI does not explicitly prohibit B2B cold email, but the purpose limitation principle means you must have a specified purpose for acquiring and using the contact's information. Using a business email address for marketing is generally permissible if the email is relevant to the recipient's professional role and includes an opt-out mechanism.
- **Opt-out**: Must provide an easy and free method for recipients to opt out of marketing communications. The APPI requires honoring opt-out requests "without delay."
- **Business card data**: In Japan, business cards (meishi — 名刺) are a primary source of contact information. The PPC has issued guidance that business card data may be used for the purpose of the business relationship under which the card was exchanged, but using it for unrelated marketing purposes may require separate consent.
- **Telemarketing**: The Act on Specified Commercial Transactions (特定商取引法) regulates telemarketing. Calls must identify the caller and purpose. No national DNC registry for B2B, but opt-out requests must be honored.

### Anti-Spam Regulations

- **Act on the Regulation of Transmission of Specified Electronic Mail (特定電子メール法)**: Japan's anti-spam law. Applies to commercial email sent to individuals (including sole proprietors). Key requirements:
  - Must include the sender's name, address, and contact information
  - Must provide an opt-out mechanism
  - Must not use deceptive subject lines
  - Prohibits sending commercial email to addresses obtained through dictionary attacks or random generation
  - Applies primarily to B2C but principles are applied to B2C-like solicitations to sole proprietors

### Sector-Specific Regulations

- **Financial Services**: FSA regulations require specific consent and disclosure for marketing financial products. Financial institutions must comply with the Banking Act, Financial Instruments and Exchange Act, and related FSA guidelines.
- **Healthcare**: The Act on the Protection of Personal Information in the Medical and Welfare Fields applies to medical institutions and health data handlers.
- **Telecommunications**: The Telecommunications Business Act requires telecommunications carriers to protect communication secrecy and customer data.

## Cultural Business Norms

Japanese business culture is among the most distinctive and formal in the world. Understanding and respecting these norms is not optional — it is a prerequisite for successful B2B engagement.

### Communication Style

- **Formal and indirect**: Japanese business communication is high-context — meaning is conveyed through context, relationships, and non-verbal cues, not just words. Direct confrontation is avoided. "Maybe" often means "no." Silence is a meaningful part of communication.
- **Keigo (敬語)**: The Japanese honorific language system with three levels: teineigo (polite), sonkeigo (respectful), and kenjōgo (humble). Using the wrong level is a serious social error. For B2B outreach, use teineigo (polite form) as a minimum; sonkeigo when addressing senior executives.
- **Nemawashi (根回し)**: The practice of building consensus informally before a formal meeting or decision. This is the single most important concept for understanding Japanese B2B sales — decisions are rarely made in meetings; they are confirmed after prior consensus-building.
- **Ringi (稟議)**: The formal approval system used in Japanese companies, where a proposal circulates through the organization for stamps (hanko) of approval from each relevant level. This process is slow (weeks to months) but creates organizational buy-in.
- **Reading the air (空気を読む — Kūki wo yomu)**: The ability to "read the atmosphere" and understand unspoken expectations. This applies to B2B interactions — a prospect's silence or vague response may signal disinterest that they consider too impolite to state directly.

### Business Card Exchange (Meishi — 名刺)

- **Ritual significance**: Business card exchange is a formal ritual, not a casual swap. Cards are presented with both hands, received with both hands, and studied carefully before being placed on the table (never pocketed immediately).
- **Rank and title**: Japanese business cards include rank indicators (e.g., 部長 buchō — department head, 課長 kachō — section chief, 係長 kakarichō — unit chief). These titles determine the level of respect and keigo required.
- **Practical implication for LeadReach**: When Bard composes outreach, the recipient's title must be researched and the appropriate keigo level used. Using first names is inappropriate in Japanese business — always use surname + title (e.g., Tanaka-buchō, not "Hi Takeshi").

### Meeting Etiquette

- **Punctuality**: Mandatory. Arriving 5 minutes early is considered on time; arriving at the scheduled time is late.
- **Seating arrangement**: Seating follows a strict protocol based on rank (kamiza — seat of honor, farthest from the door, vs. shimoza — lowest rank, closest to the door). Do not sit until invited.
- **Silence**: Normal and expected. Do not fill silences with chatter. Wait for the Japanese side to speak first in formal meetings.
- **Gift-giving (Omiyage — お土産)**: Bringing a small gift from your home country or region is expected when meeting in person. The gift should be modest (~¥3,000–¥5,000), wrapped, and presented with both hands. Never open a gift in front of the giver unless invited.
- **Nijikai (二次会)**: The "second party" after a formal dinner — a more informal drinking gathering where relationships are built. Important for relationship-building; declining may be seen as aloof.

### Decision-Making Speed

- **Among the slowest globally**: 6–18 months for enterprise B2B (12–24 months for large enterprises with ringi systems); 3–6 months for mid-market; 1–3 months for startups.
- **Consensus-driven**: Multiple layers of approval (ringi) mean that even when the economic buyer wants to proceed, they must build internal consensus. Pushing for faster decisions is counterproductive and may damage the relationship.
- **Once decided, very durable**: Japanese companies have very low vendor churn. Once trust is established and the relationship is formalized, it tends to be long-term (5–10+ year contracts are not uncommon).

### Relationship Requirements

- **Trust above all**: Japanese buyers prioritize trust, reliability, and long-term relationship potential over features and price. "Will this vendor still be here in 10 years?" is a genuine concern.
- **Local entity essential**: Having a Japanese legal entity (GK or KK) and Japanese-speaking staff is virtually mandatory for enterprise deals. Selling from overseas into Japan is possible for SMBs but extremely difficult for enterprise.
- **Reference customers**: Japanese references are critical. A reference from a US Fortune 500 is less persuasive than a reference from a comparable Japanese company.
- **Certifications**: ISO 27001 (JIS Q 27001 in Japan), ISMAP (Information System Security Management and Assessment Program — Japan's government cloud certification), and SOC 2 are important trust signals.

## Language & Localization

### Japanese Language Requirements

- **Official language**: Japanese is the sole official language. All government, legal, and most business documentation is in Japanese.
- **Written system**: Three scripts — kanji (Chinese characters), hiragana, and katakana. Romanji (Latin alphabet) is used for some brand names and international contexts.
- **Language in B2B**: Japanese is the operating language for the vast majority of Japanese businesses. Even companies with international operations conduct internal business in Japanese. English-language outreach will reach a small subset (primarily in tech, trading companies, and foreign-affiliated firms).

### Localization Imperatives

- **Japanese-language outreach is non-negotiable for most sectors**: Cold email in English to a Japanese company has a <2% response rate. Japanese-language email has a 5–15% response rate depending on quality.
- **Formality levels**: Bard must compose Japanese outreach at the correct keigo level. Using casual language (tamego) to a buchō (department head) is a fatal error. Using overly formal keigo to a startup founder may seem stiff.
- **Subject lines**: Japanese subject lines should be concise, professional, and avoid clickbait. "【ご案内】[会社名]様向け[分野]ソリューションのご提案" (【Notification】Proposal for [Company]-targeted [Field] Solution) is a typical B2B format.
- **Name order**: In Japanese, family name comes first (Tanaka Yūtarō, not Yūtarō Tanaka). Always use the Japanese order when writing in Japanese. In English, either order is acceptable, but Japanese order shows cultural awareness.
- **Product localization**: Product UI, documentation, and support must be in Japanese for enterprise sales. "English-only" products face severe headwinds in Japan.

## Tax & Financial Infrastructure

### Tax ID Formats

| Identifier | Format | Issuer | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Corporate Number (法人番号)** | 13 digits | National Tax Agency | Universal identifier; searchable at hojin.or.jp |
| **Corporate Registration Number** | Varies by jurisdiction | Legal Affairs Bureau | Regional identifier from commercial register |

### Corporate Tax Rates

- **National corporate tax**: 23.2% (standard rate for companies with capital >¥100M)
- **Local corporate taxes**: ~10–12% additional (varies by municipality)
- **Effective tax rate**: ~30–32% for large companies
- **Reduced rates**: Companies with capital ≤¥100M benefit from reduced rates on first ¥8M of income (~22% effective)
- **Consumption tax (VAT equivalent)**: 10% standard; 8% reduced (food, newspapers)

### Financial Reporting

- **Public companies**: Must file securities reports (有価証券報告書 — Yūka Shōken Hōkokusho) quarterly and annually via EDINET. Follow Japanese GAAP or IFRS (optional since 2010; ~15% of TSE-listed companies use IFRS).
- **Private companies**: Financial statements not publicly available. Must file with the Legal Affairs Bureau (available as register extracts for a fee).

### Banking & Payment Infrastructure

- **Banking**: Mitsubishi UFJ, SMBC (Sumitomo Mitsui), Mizuho, and Resona are the four mega-banks. Regional banks (100+) serve SMEs.
- **Payments**: Cash remains dominant (~70% of transactions by value). Bank transfers (furikomi — 振込) are standard for B2B. Credit card accepted for SaaS subscriptions. QR code payments (PayPay, LINE Pay) growing rapidly in B2C but limited B2B adoption.
- **Invoicing**: The Qualified Invoice System (適格請求書保存方式 — Tekikaku Seikyūsho Hozon Hōshiki), effective October 2023, requires businesses to issue and retain qualified invoices for consumption tax deductions. This is a significant compliance requirement and a B2B signal (newly registered businesses are actively adjusting their financial systems).

## ICP Considerations by Sector

### SaaS & Digital Transformation (DX)

- **Company size**: SMEs (10–500) and enterprises (1,000–50,000)
- **Decision-making**: IT department + business unit consensus; ringi approval; DX promotion officers increasingly common
- **Revenue benchmarks**: $1M–$100M for SaaS vendors; $50M–$50B for enterprise targets
- **Key signals**: Digital Agency funding, METI DX assessments, hiring for DX roles, cloud migration announcements
- **Pain points**: Legacy system modernization, paper-based processes, aging workforce knowledge transfer

### Manufacturing & Industry 4.0

- **Company size**: SMEs (50–500) to keiretsu-affiliated majors (5,000–300,000)
- **Decision-making**: Engineering-led; consensus through ringi; supplier relationship officers for keiretsu
- **Revenue benchmarks**: $10M–$50B; vast supply chain of SMEs
- **Key signals**: METI manufacturing surveys, Monodzukuri (craftsmanship) awards, industry association memberships
- **Special consideration**: Supplier qualification processes are lengthy (6–12 months); being on an approved vendor list is a major advantage

### Financial Services

- **Company size**: Mega-banks (30,000–90,000); regional banks (1,000–5,000); insurance (5,000–50,000); fintechs (10–500)
- **Decision-making**: Centralized IT governance; FSA compliance-driven; quarterly budget cycles
- **Revenue benchmarks**: $1B–$50B for major banks; $100M–$5B for regional banks
- **Key signals**: FSA regulatory changes, open banking API adoption, cashless payment initiatives, System Integration projects
- **Data residency**: FSA guidelines require Japanese data residency for financial data; ISMAP certification required for government cloud

### Gaming & Entertainment

- **Company size**: Studios (20–500), publishers (500–10,000)
- **Decision-making**: Creative leadership (producers, directors) for studios; business development for publishers
- **Revenue benchmarks**: $1M–$10B; hit-driven revenue

- **Localization**: Japanese-language support essential; Kyoto (Nintendo) and Tokyo (Sony, Bandai Namco) are primary hubs

## Outreach Compliance Checklist

### Pre-Outreach

- [ ] Verify company exists and is active using Corporate Number lookup (hojin.or.jp) or commercial registry
- [ ] Determine APPI compliance requirements — specify purpose of personal information use
- [ ] Document the purpose of use for each contact's personal information
- [ ] Prepare opt-out mechanism in every email template (must be easy and free)
- [ ] Include sender identification: company name, registered address, contact information
- [ ] Prepare Japanese-language version of all outreach materials
- [ ] Determine appropriate keigo level based on recipient's title and seniority
- [ ] For phone: prepare Japanese-language introduction script; identify caller, company, and purpose
- [ ] For financial services: confirm FSA compliance; prepare ISMAP certification if applicable
- [ ] Verify no prior opt-out from the prospect

### During Outreach

- [ ] Use correct keigo level for recipient's title and seniority
- [ ] Include sender identification (company name, address, contact info) in every email
- [ ] Include easy opt-out mechanism in every email
- [ ] Ensure email content is relevant to recipient's professional role (APPI purpose limitation)
- [ ] Do not use deceptive subject lines (Specified Electronic Mail Act)
- [ ] Honor opt-out requests immediately ("without delay")
- [ ] For phone: identify yourself, company, and purpose within first 15 seconds
- [ ] Do not call outside business hours (9am–6pm JST)
- [ ] Document all outreach attempts and purpose specification

### Post-Outreach

- [ ] Process all opt-out requests immediately
- [ ] Maintain records of purpose specifications and consent/opt-out for 5+ years
- [ ] Store outreach records for APPI compliance audit purposes
- [ ] Monitor for APPI data subject requests — respond within 30 days
- [ ] Report any data breaches to PPC within required timeframes (3–5 days for serious breaches)
- [ ] Annual review of APPI compliance documentation

### Record-Keeping Requirements

- **APPI**: Maintain records of purpose of use, consent, opt-outs, and data processing activities for the duration of processing + 3 years
- **Specified Electronic Mail Act**: Maintain records of sender identification and opt-out compliance for 1 year
- **Practical rule**: Keep all consent, purpose specification, and outreach records for **minimum 5 years** after the last interaction
