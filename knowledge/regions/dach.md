# DACH (Germany, Austria, Switzerland) — B2B Lead Intelligence & Compliance Guide

## Regional Overview

DACH — Deutschland, Austria, Confoederatio Helvetica (Germany, Austria, Switzerland) — is the largest B2B market in Europe by revenue and the most important EU expansion target for US and UK SaaS vendors. The three (technically four, including Liechtenstein) markets share a common business language (German, with Swiss and Austrian variants), deeply intertwined trade relationships, and a cultural emphasis on precision, quality, and relationship-driven procurement. Combined GDP exceeds ~$5.5T USD (Germany ~$4.5T, Switzerland ~$900B, Austria ~$520B), making DACH the world's fourth-largest economic bloc after the US, China, and Japan.

The defining characteristics of DACH B2B are: **risk-averse buyers** who demand technical due diligence, references from industry peers, and proof of compliance before engagement; **strict privacy enforcement** under GDPR (Germany, Austria) and FADP (Switzerland), supplemented by national laws (BDSG in Germany, DSG in Austria) that are often stricter than the EU baseline; **strong works councils (Betriebsrat)** in Germany and Austria that must approve any tool affecting employee behavior, including CRM, SDR tooling, and surveillance software; and **a preference for local language and local presence** that means English-only outreach to German mid-market companies will underperform by 3–5x. Sales cycles are the longest in the Western B2B world: 90–180 days for mid-market SaaS, 6–12 months for enterprise deals.

The three DACH markets are distinct despite shared language and culture. **Germany** (~84M population, 3.8M registered companies) is the largest, most regulated, and slowest-moving — dominated by the Mittelstand (family-owned SMEs that are often global market leaders in niche industrial segments) and large corporates with complex procurement processes. **Austria** (~9M, 400K companies) is smaller, more flexible, and often used as a DACH entry point due to Vienna's international orientation and less competitive market. **Switzerland** (~8.8M, 600K companies) is non-EU but GDPR-aligned (FADP revised 2023), commands the highest ACVs in DACH (~30–50% above Germany), and has extraordinary concentrations of pharma (Novartis, Roche, Lonza), finance (UBS, Zurich, Swiss Re), and high-tech (Google Zurich, ETH spinoffs). **Liechtenstein** (~39K, 15K companies) is a niche micro-state with its own registry (Handelsregister) and data protection law, relevant primarily for financial services and trust companies.

LeadReach treats DACH as a tier-1 EU expansion market — high ACVs compensate for longer cycles, and German-language capability (via Bard) is essential for credibility.

**Key facts**:
- **Combined GDP**: ~$5.5T USD (2025); Germany ~$4.5T, Switzerland ~$900B, Austria ~$520B
- **Population**: ~102M; Germany 84M, Switzerland 8.8M, Austria 9.1M, Liechtenstein 39K
- **Currencies**: EUR (Germany, Austria), CHF (Switzerland, Liechtenstein)
- **Government**: Germany — federal parliamentary republic (16 Bundesländer); Austria — federal parliamentary republic (9 Bundesländer); Switzerland — federal semi-direct democracy (26 cantons); Liechtenstein — constitutional monarchy
- **Languages**: German (official in all four; Swiss Standard German for written, Swiss German dialect for spoken in CH); French (western Switzerland — Geneva, Vaud, Neuchâtel, Jura); Italian (Ticino, southern Graubünden); Romansh (Graubünden, CH); English widely spoken in tech/finance in all markets
- **Time zones**: CET (UTC+1, all four); CEST (UTC+2, summer); Switzerland same as Germany
- **Internet penetration**: ~93% (DE), ~92% (AT), ~96% (CH)
- **Business centers**: Berlin (tech, startups), Munich (manufacturing, automotive, insurance), Frankfurt (finance, fintech), Hamburg (media, logistics), Stuttgart (automotive, engineering), Zurich (finance, pharma, high-tech), Basel (pharma, life sciences), Vienna (financial services, international orgs)

## Business Registration Infrastructure

DACH business registration is highly structured and legally rigorous, but fragmented across three (four) jurisdictions. There is no single DACH-wide registry — each country maintains its own system, and within Germany, registration is further decentralized to local courts (Amtsgerichte).

### Germany — Handelsregister & Bundesamt

- **Handelsregister** (Commercial Register): Maintained by local courts (Amtsgericht) across ~400 registry districts. Every commercial business (GmbH, AG, KG, OHG, etc.) must be registered. Data includes: company name, legal form, registered office, managing directors (Geschäftsführer for GmbH), board members (Vorstand for AG), share capital, registered provisions, and filings.
- **Bundesamt für Justiz** (Federal Office of Justice): Maintains the central register for associations (Vereinsregister) and the transparency register (Transparenzregister) for beneficial ownership.
- **Gemeinsames Registerportal**: The joint register portal (`handelsregister.de`) provides a unified search interface across German commercial registers. **Paid** — searches cost ~€2–5 per query; detailed extracts (Handelsregisterauszug) cost ~€10–15 per document.
- **Unternehmensregister** (`unternehmensregister.de`): The official electronic federal gazette (Bundesanzeiger) platform. Publishes annual financial statements, insolvency notices, and other mandatory filings for German companies. **Partially free** — basic filings free; detailed financials often require payment.
- **Bundesanstalt für Finanzdienstleistungsaufsicht (BaFin)**: Financial services register — lists all licensed banks, insurers, and investment firms in Germany. Searchable at `bafin.de`.
- **Transparenzregister** (Transparency Register): Germany's beneficial ownership register, maintained by the Bundesanzeiger Verlag. Since 2022, all associations (Vereine), trusts, and companies must report beneficial owners. Accessible to competent authorities and, with restrictions, to the public.
- **Company types**: GmbH (Gesellschaft mit beschränkter Haftung — most common, min €25K share capital); AG (Aktiengesellschaft — public company, min €50K); UG (Unternehmergesellschaft — "mini-GmbH," min €1); KG (Kommanditgesellschaft — limited partnership); OHG (Offene Handelsgesellschaft — general partnership); e.K. (Einzelkaufmann — sole proprietorship); GbR (Gesellschaft bürgerlichen Rechts — civil law partnership, no registration required).

### Austria — Firmenbuch

- **Firmenbuch** (Company Register): Austria's central commercial register, maintained by the Federal Ministry of Justice. Searchable online at `firmenbuch.gv.at` — **paid** for detailed extracts (Firmenbuchauszug), ~€3–10 per query.
- **Firmenbuchnummer (FN)**: Unique company identifier in the Firmenbuch (e.g., FN 123456x). Essential for verifying Austrian companies.
- **ZVR-Zahl**: Central register number for associations (Vereine).
- **Bundesministerium für Digitalisierung und Wirtschaftsstandort (BMDW)**: Oversees business registration and economic policy.
- **Wirtschaftskammer Österreich (WKO)**: Austrian Federal Economic Chamber — every business in Austria must be a member. WKO maintains a comprehensive business database searchable at `wko.at` — **free** basic search.
- **Company types**: GmbH (min €35K share capital); AG (min €70K); OG (Offene Gesellschaft — general partnership); KG (Kommanditgesellschaft); GmbH & Co KG (limited partnership with GmbH as general partner — extremely common in Austria); Einzelunternehmen (sole proprietorship).

### Switzerland — Zefix & Handelsregister

- **Zefix** (Zentrales Firmenindex): Switzerland's central business name index, maintained by the Federal Office of Justice. Searchable at `zefix.ch` — **free** basic search (company name, UID, legal form, canton, status). Detailed extracts redirect to the cantonal commercial register.
- **UID (Unternehmens-Identifikationsnummer)**: Switzerland's unique business identifier, issued by the Federal Statistical Office. Format: CHE-123.456.789. Every company registered in the commercial register receives a UID.
- **Cantonal Handelsregister**: Each of Switzerland's 26 cantons maintains its own commercial register. Zefix provides the index; detailed data (directors, share capital, articles of association) is obtained from the relevant cantonal registry. **Paid** — extract costs vary by canton (~CHF 20–50).
- **SHAB (Schweizerisches Handelsamtsblatt)**: Swiss Official Gazette of Commerce — publishes all mandatory company filings (incorporation, changes, insolvency). Searchable at `shab.ch` — **free** search, paid for detailed documents.
- **Company types**: AG (Aktiengesellschaft — min CHF 100K share capital); GmbH (min CHF 20K); Einzelfirma (sole proprietorship); Kollektivgesellschaft (general partnership); Kommanditgesellschaft (limited partnership); Verein (association).

### Liechtenstein — Handelsregister

- **Handelsregister** (Commercial Register): Maintained by the Office of Justice (Justizdienststelle). Searchable at `hr.llv.li` — **paid** for extracts.
- **Company types**: AG (min CHF 50K), GmbH (min CHF 10K), Anstalt (establishment — unique to Liechtenstein), Stiftung (foundation), Trust. Liechtenstein is known for its trust and foundation structures, which are relevant for financial services prospecting.
- **LIE-tax**: Liechtenstein uses the Swiss franc (CHF) as its currency and has a customs and monetary union with Switzerland.

## Regulatory & Compliance Framework

DACH is the most heavily regulated B2B environment in the Western world. The baseline is GDPR (Germany, Austria) or FADP (Switzerland), supplemented by national laws that are often stricter. For LeadReach, compliance design must account for three overlapping layers: EU/national privacy law, electronic communications law, and works council/employee protection law.

### GDPR (EU) — Applies to Germany & Austria

GDPR is the baseline privacy law for Germany and Austria. Key requirements for B2B outreach:

- **Lawful basis**: Six lawful bases (consent, contract, legal obligation, vital interests, public task, legitimate interest). B2B cold email generally falls under **legitimate interest (Article 6(1)(f))**, but requires a documented **Legitimate Interest Assessment (LIA)** — a balancing test weighing the seller's interest against the data subject's rights.
- **Data Subject Rights (DSARs)**: Access, rectification, erasure, portability, restriction, objection to processing. Must respond within 30 days.
- **Data Processing Agreement (DPA)**: Article 28 requires a contract with any vendor processing personal data on your behalf. Standard Contractual Clauses (AVV — Auftragsverarbeitungsvertrag in Germany) are expected.
- **Breach notification**: 72 hours to the supervisory authority (BfDI in Germany, DSB in Austria).
- **Records of Processing Activities (RoPA)**: Article 30 documentation required for organizations with >250 employees or those processing sensitive data regularly.
- **Penalties**: Up to **€20M or 4% of global annual revenue**, whichever is higher.

### German Federal Data Protection Act (BDSG)

BDSG supplements GDPR with stricter German-specific provisions that go beyond the EU baseline:

- **Employee data protection** (§26 BDSG): Stricter consent requirements for processing employee data. Employee consent for monitoring, tracking, or analytics must be "voluntary" — German courts and DPAs interpret this narrowly given the power imbalance in employment.
- **DPO (Datenschutzbeauftragter)**: Mandatory for any company with **>20 employees** regularly processing personal data (lower threshold than GDPR's suggestion of public authorities or large-scale processing). The DPO must be designated in writing and can be an internal employee or external service provider.
- **Works Council (Betriebsrat) co-determination (Mitbestimmung)**: Under the Betriebsverfassungsgesetz (BetrVG), the works council has **mandatory co-determination rights** (zwingende Mitbestimmung, §87 BetrVG) for any technical system that affects employee behavior, performance, or monitoring — including CRM systems, SDR tools, email tracking, keyloggers, and even badge access systems. **This is the single most common deal-blocker for SaaS vendors selling into German companies with >5 employees.** The works council can delay or block tool adoption for months.
- **Right to informational self-determination**: Derived from the German Constitution (Grundgesetz Art. 2(1) + Art. 1(1)), this fundamental right influences all data protection interpretation in Germany and makes German DPAs among the strictest in the EU.

### TTDSG — Telekommunikation-Telemedien-Datenschutz-Gesetz (Germany)

TTDSG (effective 2021, replacing the Telemediengesetz and TKG data protection provisions) governs electronic communications metadata and tracking:

- **Tracking pixels in cold email are illegal without consent** — this is a critical difference from US/UK practice. Open-tracking, click-tracking, and device fingerprinting all require prior consent under TTDSG §25.
- **Cookie consent**: Same as ePrivacy Directive — consent required for non-essential cookies.
- **Breach notification**: Separate from GDPR breach notification; telecom-specific breaches reported to BNetzA (Federal Network Agency).

### Austrian Data Protection Act (DSG)

Austria's Datenschutzgesetz (DSG) supplements GDPR with Austrian-specific provisions:

- **DPO mandatory** for organizations processing data subject to DSG provisions and regularly employing >20 people in data processing (similar to Germany).
- **Works council (Betriebsrat)**: Same co-determination rights as Germany under the Arbeitsverfassungsgesetz (ArbVG). Must approve any IT system affecting employees.
- **Austrian DSB (Datenschutzbehörde)**: Austria's supervisory authority — generally more pragmatic than German DPAs but still strict on employee data and tracking.

### Swiss FADP (Federal Act on Data Protection, revised 2023)

Switzerland's revised FADP (nFADP) came into effect on September 1, 2023, aligning Swiss data protection law more closely with GDPR:

- **Scope**: Applies to the processing of personal data by private bodies and federal bodies in Switzerland. Extraterritorial application to foreign entities processing data of persons in Switzerland.
- **Cross-border data transfer**: Requires adequate level of protection in the receiving country (adequacy decision by the FDPIC) or appropriate safeguards (contractual, binding corporate rules). Switzerland has a mutual adequacy decision with the EU.
- **Swiss DPO**: Recommended for companies with >50 employees processing sensitive data; not mandatory but considered best practice.
- **Breach notification**: Mandatory to the FDPIC (Federal Data Protection and Information Commissioner) for data breaches likely to result in a high risk to the personality or fundamental rights of data subjects.
- **Penalties**: Intentional violations can result in fines up to CHF 250,000. The nFADP introduces criminal liability for individuals in some cases.
- **Important**: Switzerland is NOT in the EU/EEA — GDPR does not directly apply. However, the EU has granted Switzerland an adequacy decision, and Swiss companies processing EU residents' data must comply with GDPR.

### Liechtenstein Data Protection Act (DSG-L)

Liechtenstein's data protection law aligns closely with the Swiss FADP and, through the EEA Agreement, with GDPR for certain contexts. The Data Protection Authority (Datenschutzstelle) oversees enforcement.

### Impressum Requirement (Germany, Austria)

German law (formerly Telemediengesetz, now DDG — Digitale-Dienste-Gesetz) and Austrian law require an **Impressum** (legal notice / imprint) on all commercial communications including emails, websites, and social media profiles. Must include:

- Full legal name of the company
- Legal form (GmbH, AG, etc.)
- Registered office address
- Contact information (email, phone)
- Commercial register number and registering court
- VAT identification number (USt-IdNr.)
- Managing directors / board members (names)

**Omitting the Impressum is a cease-and-desist risk (Abmahnung)** — competitors and specialized law firms actively scan for non-compliance and send costly cease-and-desist letters. This is a uniquely German/Austrian compliance requirement that must be included in every Bard-generated email for DACH.

### Anti-Spam Law (UWG — Germany)

Germany's Gesetz gegen den unlauteren Wettbewerb (UWG, Act Against Unfair Competition) governs commercial communications beyond email:

- **§7 UWG**: Unacceptable nuisance — covers unsolicited phone calls, emails, faxes. B2B cold email is generally permissible under legitimate interest (GDPR) if the product is relevant to the recipient's role. Cold calls to consumers require prior consent; B2B calls are more permissive but must respect opt-outs.
- **Robinson list** (Gewerblicher Verband für direkte Wirtschaftskommunikation e.V.): Germans can register to opt out of direct marketing. Scrubbing is voluntary but recommended.

## Cultural Business Norms

DACH business culture is relationship-driven, formal, and risk-averse. Understanding these norms is not optional — it is the difference between a 3% response rate and a 15% response rate.

### Communication Style

- **Formal and precise**: German business communication values accuracy and detail over brevity. A 300-word email explaining your solution in detail will outperform a 50-word US-style pitch. Do not mistake German directness for rudeness — "Das funktioniert nicht" (That won't work) is a factual observation, not a personal rejection.
- **Du vs. Sie**: In German business, the formal "Sie" is the default. Never use "du" unless explicitly invited (this is called the "Bruderschaft trinken" — drinking to brotherhood — tradition). In written outreach, always use "Sie."
- **Written formality**: Letters, emails, and proposals should follow formal German business letter conventions. "Sehr geehrte(r) Frau/Herr [Last Name]" for formal; "Guten Tag [Last Name]" for semi-formal.
- **Understatement preferred**: Avoid superlatives ("revolutionary," "best-in-class," "world-class"). German buyers are skeptical of marketing hype. Lead with technical specifications, certifications, and measurable outcomes.

### Meeting Etiquette

- **Punctuality is non-negotiable**: Being 5 minutes late to a meeting (even virtual) is considered disrespectful. Arrive 2–3 minutes early.
- **Agenda required**: German meetings follow a structured agenda. Sending an agenda 24–48 hours before the meeting is expected. Side-tracking is discouraged.
- **Decision meetings vs. information meetings**: Germans distinguish between "Besprechung" (discussion) and "Entscheidung" (decision). Do not expect decisions in the first meeting — the first meeting is for information gathering.
- **Titles matter**: Academic and professional titles (Dr., Prof., Dipl.-Ing.) are used in Germany and Austria. Using a prospect's title correctly is a sign of respect. In Switzerland, titles are less emphasized.
- **Handshakes**: Pre-COVID, firm handshakes were standard. Post-COVID, virtual greetings are acceptable for initial meetings. In-person meetings still favor handshakes.

### Decision-Making Speed

- **Slow and consensus-driven**: German organizations, particularly larger ones, involve multiple stakeholders (technical, legal, procurement, works council, DPO) early in the process. Decisions are made collectively, not unilaterally by a champion.
- **Typical timelines**: Mid-market SaaS — 90–180 days; Enterprise — 6–12 months; Public sector — 12–24 months (VgV — Vergabeverordnung procurement rules).
- **The "German yes"**: When a German buyer says "yes" or "we will proceed," they mean it — the decision has been through all internal gates. Conversely, "we need to discuss internally" is not a polite no — it genuinely means further internal discussion is needed.

### Relationship Requirements

- **Trust is earned slowly**: German business relationships develop over multiple interactions. Expect 3–5 touchpoints before a meeting is granted, and 2–3 meetings before a deal progresses.
- **References are essential**: German buyers demand references from **peers in their industry** — a reference from a US tech company is far less persuasive than a reference from a German company in the same sector. LeadReach should prioritize German references in outreach.
- **Local presence matters**: A German address, German-language support, and a German legal entity (or at minimum a German-language website with Impressum) significantly improve trust. Swiss buyers similarly value Swiss presence.
- **Trade fair culture**: DACH has the world's most active trade fair calendar. Hannover Messe, DMEXCO, IAA Mobility, MEDICA, CeBIT (defunct but its audience migrated), Bauma, and Automechanika are key relationship-building events. Post-event follow-up is a primary B2B channel.

### Gift-Giving

- **Modest and appropriate**: Small gifts (quality pens, regional specialties, company-branded items) are acceptable at the conclusion of a deal or during trade fairs. Lavish gifts (perceived as bribes) are not only culturally inappropriate but may violate criminal law (§299 StGB — bribery in commercial practice).
- **Holiday cards**: Christmas cards and calendars are common and appreciated in established business relationships.

### Hierarchy

- **Formal hierarchy**: German and Austrian organizations tend to have clearer hierarchies than US/UK companies. Decisions flow upward. Junior employees are not empowered to make purchasing decisions.
- **Mittelstand exception**: Many Mittelstand companies are family-owned and the Geschäftsführer (CEO/Managing Director) makes decisions directly — but still slowly, with consultation.
- **Swiss pragmatism**: Swiss organizations are less hierarchical than German/Austrian ones but still value seniority and expertise.

## Language & Localization

### German — The Default Business Language

- **Germany**: German is the dominant business language outside of international tech companies and startups. **English-only outreach to mid-market German companies will underperform by 3–5x** — not because Germans can't read English (most can), but because German-language outreach signals commitment to the market and respect for local norms.
- **Austria**: German (Austrian variant — some vocabulary differences: "Marille" not "Aprikose," "Faschiertes" not "Hackfleisch," but these rarely affect B2B communication). Vienna business culture is more international than the rest of Austria; English is more acceptable.
- **Switzerland**: **Swiss Standard German** (Schweizer Hochdeutsch) for written communication — this is the formal written standard, nearly identical to Standard German with minor spelling/vocabulary differences. **Swiss German** (Schweizerdeutsch) for spoken communication — a dialect that is significantly different from Standard German and not easily understood by non-Swiss. **Never write in Swiss German dialect** for business communication — use Standard German or English.
- **Western Switzerland**: French-speaking (Geneva, Lausanne, Neuchâtel, Fribourg, Valais). Outreach to these regions should be in French.
- **Ticino**: Italian-speaking. Outreach should be in Italian.

### English Acceptability

- **Berlin startups**: English is the working language; English-only outreach is acceptable.
- **Frankfurt finance**: English is widely spoken but German outreach is preferred.
- **Munich/Switzerland enterprise**: German preferred for initial contact; English acceptable for technical content.
- **Rule of thumb**: If the prospect's LinkedIn profile or company website is primarily in English, use English. Otherwise, default to German (or French for western Switzerland).

### Localization Best Practices

- **German spelling**: Use new German spelling rules (Rechtschreibreform). Common errors: "ss" vs "ß" (use "ß" after long vowels and diphthongs).
- **Formal address**: Always use "Sie" (formal you) in written outreach until the prospect switches to "du."
- **Impressum**: Must be included in every commercial email sent to Germany/Austria — non-negotiable.
- **Currency**: Quote in EUR for Germany/Austria; CHF for Switzerland. Never quote DACH prospects in USD.

- **VAT rate**: 8.1% standard (increased from 7.7% in 2024); 2.6% reduced; 3.8% for accommodation
- **Financial reporting**: Swiss GAAP FER or IFRS for listed companies. Smaller companies use Swiss GAAP or ER (Ehrenhaft).
- **Swiss VAT reverse charge**: B2B services from outside Switzerland are subject to reverse charge — buyer self-assesses VAT.

## ICP Considerations by Sector

### Automotive & Manufacturing

- **Company size**: Tier 1 suppliers (10K–100K employees), Tier 2 (500–10K), Tier 3 (50–500)
- **Decision-making**: Highly formalized procurement (RFQ/I process); long evaluation; quality and certification (TISAX, ISO 27001, IATF 16949) are table stakes
- **Revenue benchmarks**: Tier 1 — €5B–€50B; Tier 2 — €100M–€5B; Tier 3 — €5M–€100M
- **Key signals**: TISAX certification, IAA participation, digital transformation press releases, budget announcements (typically Q4 for following year)
- **Works council**: Very common in automotive/manufacturing — plan for Betriebsrat approval in sales cycle

### Pharma & Life Sciences

- **Company size**: Big pharma (50K–120K), mid-tier (1K–10K), biotech (10–500)
- **Decision-making**: Scientifically rigorous; validation-heavy; long procurement (6–12 months for enterprise)
- **Revenue benchmarks**: Big pharma — €20B–€60B; mid-tier — €500M–€5B; biotech — pre-revenue to €500M
- **Key signals**: Clinical trial milestones, EMA/FDA filings, GxP compliance requirements, MEDICA participation
- **Data residency**: Strong preference for EU hosting; Swiss pharma requires Swiss hosting

### Fintech & Financial Services

- **Company size**: Banks (5K–100K), fintechs (20–500), insurers (1K–50K)
- **Decision-making**: Compliance-driven; BaFin/FINMA review for vendor appointments; DPO and procurement involved
- **Revenue benchmarks**: Major banks — €10B–€40B; fintechs — €1M–€500M
- **Key signals**: BaFin/FINMA announcements, PSD2 compliance projects, fintech sandbox enrollment, regulatory technology investments
- **Outsourcing rules**: BaFin requires notification for material outsourcing (AT 9 — Auslagerung); FINMA has equivalent rules

### Mittelstand (SME / Hidden Champions)

- **Company size**: 50–3,000 employees; often family-owned, multi-generational
- **Decision-making**: Geschäftsführer (CEO) makes decisions; consults with internal stakeholders but more streamlined than large corporate
- **Revenue benchmarks**: €10M–€500M; many are global market leaders in niche segments
- **Key signals**: Trade fair participation, IHK membership, export awards, Industry 4.0 initiative announcements
- **Relationship**: Long-term trusted partner > best-of-breed vendor; local presence and German-language support are differentiators

## Outreach Compliance Checklist

### Pre-Outreach

- [ ] Document Legitimate Interest Assessment (LIA) for B2B email — weigh your interest against the data subject's rights
- [ ] Verify company exists and is active in Handelsregister (DE), Firmenbuch (AT), or Zefix (CH)
- [ ] Determine if works council (Betriebsrat) exists at the target company — if so, factor into sales cycle
- [ ] Prepare Impressum for all emails targeting Germany and Austria
- [ ] Ensure no tracking pixels or open/click tracking in cold emails (TTDSG, DE)
- [ ] Prepare German-language outreach (or French for western Switzerland)
- [ ] Verify Swiss prospects' language region (German, French, or Italian)
- [ ] Confirm DPA/AVV template is ready (Article 28 GDPR) for when the prospect requests it
- [ ] Prepare sub-processor list and data processing documentation
- [ ] Check if target sector requires TISAX (automotive), BaFin notification (finance), or other certifications

### During Outreach

- [ ] Include Impressum in every email to Germany/Austria (full legal name, address, register number, VAT ID, directors)
- [ ] Include clear opt-out mechanism in every email
- [ ] Use "Sie" (formal) in all German-language outreach
- [ ] Do not use tracking pixels, open tracking, or click tracking without consent
- [ ] Identify yourself and your company at the start of any phone call
- [ ] Do not make unsubstantiated claims — German buyers and regulators take misleading statements seriously (UWG §5)
- [ ] Provide German-language privacy policy link in email footer
- [ ] For Switzerland: include data protection notice in compliance with FADP

### Post-Outreach

- [ ] Process opt-out requests immediately (within 24 hours as best practice; 30 days maximum under GDPR)
- [ ] Maintain records of LIA, consent (if obtained), and all outreach for **minimum 5 years**
- [ ] If a DSAR is received, respond within 30 days (GDPR) or "without delay" (FADP)
- [ ] Document any data breaches and notify supervisory authority within 72 hours (GDPR) or "as soon as possible" (FADP)
- [ ] Keep sub-processor list current and available
- [ ] Monitor for Betriebsrat inquiries — respond promptly and provide requested documentation (privacy assessment, employee impact analysis)
