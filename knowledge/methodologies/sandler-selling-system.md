# Sandler Selling System — Pressure-Free Qualification and Buyer-Sychology for AI Sales

## Executive Summary

The Sandler Selling System, developed by David Sandler in 1967 and refined over five decades, turns traditional sales logic on its head. Where most methodologies teach sellers to push, persuade, and overcome objections, Sandler teaches sellers to pull, qualify, and let the prospect persuade themselves. The system's fundamental principle: **the buyer should do most of the talking, most of the selling, and most of the convincing — not the seller.**

This inversion is not semantic — it is structural. Sandler-trained reps close 30% more deals than untrained reps (Sandler Research Institute, 2025) not because they are more persuasive, but because they disqualify bad deals faster, invest time only in qualified opportunities, and create conditions where the prospect wants to buy rather than feels sold to. The system's seven-stage framework (Bonding & Rapport → Up-Front Contract → Pain Funnel → Budget → Decision → Fulfillment → Post-Sell) provides a complete selling system from first contact to post-close reinforcement.

Within the LeadReach platform, the Sandler system is particularly powerful because it addresses the two most common AI-assisted selling failures: (1) AI agents that are too accommodating and agreeable, creating order-taker dynamics rather than equal-business-standing conversations; and (2) pipeline pollution from deals that should have been disqualified early but were advanced because the system rewarded activity over qualification. The Judge agent implements Sandler qualification gates, the Bard agent crafts Sandler-style outreach that positions the seller as an equal, and the Echo agent tracks Sandler-specific metrics like disqualification rates and budget conversation timing.

## Core Methodology

### The Sandler Submarine — Seven Stages

The Sandler system is visualized as a submarine, where each compartment must be sealed (completed) before moving to the next. If water (unresolved issues) leaks into one compartment, you don't proceed — you go back and fix it.

```
┌─────────┐  ┌──────────────┐  ┌───────────┐  ┌──────┐  ┌──────────┐  ┌────────────┐  ┌───────────┐
│ Bonding │─▶│ Up-Front     │─▶│ Pain      │─▶│Budget│─▶│ Decision │─▶│Fulfillment │─▶│ Post-Sell │
│ & Rapport│ │ Contract     │  │ Funnel    │  │      │  │          │  │            │  │           │
└─────────┘  └──────────────┘  └───────────┘  └──────┘  └──────────┘  └────────────┘  └───────────┘

Each stage must be "sealed" before advancing. No shortcuts.
```

### Stage 1: Bonding & Rapport

**Definition**: Establishing a connection with the prospect where they feel heard, respected, and comfortable being honest. In Sandler, rapport is not about being friendly — it is about being genuine and creating an environment where the prospect drops their "buyer defense shield."

**The Buyer Defense Shield**: Every prospect enters a sales conversation with their guard up. They expect the seller to push, persuade, and manipulate. The defense shield manifests as:
- Guarded answers ("We're doing fine")
- Vague commitments ("Let me think about it")
- Control behaviors (dominating the conversation, setting all the terms)
- Withholding information (not sharing budget, timeline, or real problems)

**Sandler Rapport Principles:**

| Principle | Description | Implementation |
|-----------|-------------|---------------|
| Matching and Mirroring | Subtly match the prospect's communication style, pace, and energy level | If they speak slowly and formally, match that. If they're fast and casual, match that. |
| Active Listening | Demonstrate genuine understanding before advancing | Repeat back what you heard: "So what you're saying is..." |
| Equal Business Stance | Position yourself as a peer, not a subordinate or a superior | You are an expert consultant evaluating whether you can help — not a vendor begging for a meeting |
| Pattern Interrupt | Break the prospect's expected sales script to create genuine engagement | Instead of "How are you today?" → "Before we get into it, let me be upfront about how these conversations typically go..." |
| Vulnerability First | Share something genuine to invite the prospect to do the same | "I'll be honest — I don't know yet if what we do is right for you. That's what we need to figure out together." |

**Critical Sandler Insight**: In most sales conversations, the seller is in the one-down position (they want something from the buyer). Sandler flips this: the seller is in the one-up position because they have expertise the buyer needs. The buyer should be trying to qualify the seller, not the other way around.

### Stage 2: Up-Front Contract

**Definition**: A mutual agreement established at the beginning of every conversation (and every subsequent conversation) that defines the purpose, expected outcomes, ground rules, and next steps. The up-front contract eliminates ambiguity and creates psychological safety for both parties.

**Why This Matters**: 70% of sales call frustration comes from mismatched expectations — the seller expects to advance the deal, the prospect expects an informational conversation, and both leave disappointed. The up-front contract prevents this.

**Up-Front Contract Structure:**

| Element | Purpose | Example Language |
|---------|---------|-----------------|
| Purpose | Define why both parties are investing time | "The purpose of our conversation today is to understand whether there's a fit between your needs and what we do." |
| Time | Confirm and honor the time commitment | "I have 30 minutes on my calendar. Is that still working for you?" |
| Agenda | Set the conversation structure | "I'd like to cover three things: [topic 1], [topic 2], and [topic 3]. Does that work, or is there something else you'd like to cover?" |
| Outcome | Define what success looks like for the conversation | "At the end of our conversation, we'll decide together whether it makes sense to take a next step. Fair?" |
| Permission | Get explicit consent for the conversation format | "Is it okay if I ask some direct questions? I find that's the most efficient way to figure out if we can help." |
| Out | Give the prospect an explicit exit | "If at any point it doesn't look like we're a fit, I'll tell you. And I'd appreciate if you'd do the same. Deal?" |

**Up-Front Contract for Cold Outreach (Written):**

In email and LinkedIn outreach, the up-front contract is compressed but still present:

> "I have an idea that might help [specific outcome] — or it might not be relevant. Can I share it in a 15-minute call? If it doesn't resonate, we'll end the call — no hard feelings."

This language works because it:
1. States the purpose (share an idea)
2. Sets the time expectation (15 minutes)
3. Gives the prospect an explicit out (no hard feelings)
4. Removes pressure (might not be relevant)
5. Positions the seller as evaluating fit, not pushing a product

### Stage 3: Pain Funnel

**Definition**: A structured questioning technique that moves from surface-level complaints to deep, emotional, quantified business pain. The Pain Funnel is Sandler's equivalent of SPIN's Problem and Implication stages combined — but with a more deliberate depth-first approach.

**The Pain Funnel Structure:**

```
            ┌──────────────────────────┐
            │  SURFACE COMPLAINT       │  "Our SDRs aren't productive enough"
            │  (What they say first)   │
            └────────────┬─────────────┘
                         │ "Tell me more about that"
            ┌────────────▼─────────────┐
            │  SYMPTOMS                │  "They spend too much time on research"
            │  (Observable signs)      │
            └────────────┬─────────────┘
                         │ "How long has that been going on?"
            ┌────────────▼─────────────┐
            │  ROOT CAUSE              │  "We don't have good data or automation"
            │  (Why it's happening)    │
            └────────────┬─────────────┘
                         │ "Why haven't you fixed it?"
            ┌────────────▼─────────────┐
            │  PERSONAL IMPACT         │  "I'm under pressure to hit team quota"
            │  (How it affects them)   │
            └────────────┬─────────────┘
                         │ "What happens if you don't solve this?"
            ┌────────────▼─────────────┐
            │  QUANTIFIED PAIN         │  "We're missing $500K in pipeline/quarter"
            │  (The dollar figure)     │
            └──────────────────────────┘
```

**Pain Funnel Question Sequence:**

| Level | Question Type | Example Questions | Purpose |
|-------|--------------|-------------------|---------|
| 1 | Surface | "What's your biggest challenge with [area] right now?" / "What prompted you to take this call?" | Get the initial complaint on the table |
| 2 | Elaboration | "Tell me more about that." / "Can you help me understand what that looks like day-to-day?" | Unpack the surface complaint into specific symptoms |
| 3 | Duration | "How long has this been going on?" / "When did you first notice this was a problem?" | Establish chronicity — a 2-year problem is more painful than a 2-week problem |
| 4 | Impact | "How is that affecting your results?" / "What's the downstream impact of [symptom]?" | Move from symptoms to business consequences |
| 5 | Personal | "How does that affect you personally?" / "What does this mean for your goals this year?" | Connect the pain to the individual — personal pain drives action faster than organizational pain |
| 6 | Attempted Fix | "What have you tried so far?" / "Why didn't that work?" | Understand their solution history and demonstrate that partial fixes have failed |
| 7 | Cost | "What's this costing you?" / "If you don't solve this, what's the financial impact?" | Quantify the pain in dollars — this is the foundation for Budget conversations |
| 8 | Commitment | "Is this something you're committed to fixing?" / "On a scale of 1-10, how important is it to solve this in the next quarter?" | Test whether the pain is severe enough to drive action |

**The 3:1 Rule**: For every problem the prospect states, ask at least three follow-up questions before moving on. Surface-level problems are almost never the real pain. The real pain is three questions deep.

### Stage 4: Budget

**Definition**: Determining whether the prospect has the financial resources and willingness to invest in solving their identified pain. In Sandler, the Budget conversation happens *after* Pain is established — never before.

**Why Sequence Matters**: If you discuss budget before establishing pain, the conversation becomes about price. If you establish pain first, the conversation becomes about value. A prospect who has quantified a $500K/quarter problem is far more receptive to a $100K solution than one who hasn't felt the pain yet.

**Sandler Budget Questions:**

| Question | Purpose | Anti-Pattern |
|----------|---------|-------------|
| "Now that we understand the problem, is there budget allocated to solve it?" | Determine budget existence | Asking "What's your budget?" before establishing pain |
| "What range of investment would make sense given the impact we discussed?" | Anchor to value, not to the prospect's number | Letting the prospect anchor to a low number |
| "Is the investment something you'd need to get approved, or do you have authority?" | Understand the budget authority chain | Assuming the person you're talking to controls the budget |
| "If the investment required to solve this problem was [range], would that be feasible?" | Test a specific range without committing to a price | Giving a specific price before testing the range |
| "What happens if the solution costs more than expected — is there flexibility?" | Understand budget rigidity | Assuming budget is fixed (it often isn't for the right solution) |

**The Budget Rule**: Never discuss your price until you've established the prospect's pain, confirmed they have budget, and agreed on the value of solving the problem. Price discussed before value is always "too expensive."

### Stage 5: Decision

**Definition**: Understanding the complete decision-making process — who decides, how they decide, when they decide, and what criteria they use. In Sandler, the Decision stage is about mapping the entire decision architecture, not just identifying the decision-maker.

**Sandler Decision Questions:**

| Question | Purpose | Deeper Follow-Up |
|----------|---------|-----------------|
| "Beyond you, who else is involved in a decision like this?" | Map the buying committee | "And what does [stakeholder] typically care about?" |
| "Walk me through the process from 'yes, we want this' to a signed contract." | Understand the administrative process | "How long does each step take?" |
| "When you've made similar decisions before, what does the timeline look like?" | Set realistic time expectations | "What caused delays in those previous decisions?" |
| "What could cause this decision to not happen?" | Surface risks and objections | "Has that happened before?" |
| "If you decide this is the right solution, what's the next step?" | Identify the immediate action | "And then what happens after that?" |

**The Decision Rule**: If you don't know the complete decision process by the end of the second meeting, you're not in control of the deal — the prospect is.

### Stage 6: Fulfillment

**Definition**: Presenting your solution only after Pain, Budget, and Decision have been fully qualified. Fulfillment is where most sellers start (by pitching their product) and where Sandler sellers finish (by showing how their solution addresses the specific, quantified pain the prospect described).

**The Fulfillment Rule**: Only present the parts of your solution that are relevant to the prospect's stated pain. Every feature you show that doesn't address their specific problem dilutes your presentation and gives them reasons to say no.

**Fulfillment Structure:**

| Step | Description | Language Pattern |
|------|-------------|-----------------|
| 1. Pain Recap | Restate the prospect's quantified pain in their words | "You mentioned [their pain, their words] costing [their number]..." |
| 2. Solution Bridge | Connect the pain to your solution's specific capability | "Here's how we address exactly that..." |
| 3. Focused Demo | Show only the capabilities relevant to their pain | "I'm going to focus on [specific capability] because that's directly tied to the problem we discussed" |
| 4. Value Confirmation | Ask the prospect to confirm the value | "Based on what you've seen, do you believe this could [solve their problem]?" |
| 5. Budget Check | Confirm the investment is still feasible | "Given what we've covered, is the investment range we discussed still reasonable?" |
| 6. Decision Step | Propose the specific next step in the decision process | "The next step in your process is [step they described]. Can we schedule that for [date]?" |

### Stage 7: Post-Sell

**Definition**: Reinforcing the buying decision after the contract is signed. This prevents buyer's remorse, reduces churn, and creates the foundation for expansion and referrals.

**Post-Sell Questions:**

| Question | Purpose |
|----------|---------|
| "Now that you've made this decision, how do you feel about it?" | Surface any lingering hesitation |
| "Is there anything that could change your mind about this?" | Identify and address potential buyer's remorse triggers |
| "What's the first thing you want to accomplish once we get started?" | Reinforce forward momentum |
| "Who else in your organization should know about what we're doing?" | Identify expansion and referral opportunities |

## Sandler Communication Techniques

### The Reversing Technique

**Definition**: Answering a prospect's question with a question to gain more information, maintain control, and avoid falling into the seller trap.

**Why It Works**: When a prospect asks a question, the natural seller response is to answer immediately. This puts the seller in the subordinate position (answering to the buyer) and often provides information that the prospect uses to disqualify you. Reversing puts you back in control and uncovers the real intent behind the question.

**Reversing Patterns:**

| Prospect Question | Naive Answer (Wrong) | Reversed Response (Right) | Why Reversing Works |
|-------------------|---------------------|--------------------------|---------------------|
| "How much does it cost?" | "$48K per year" | "That depends on a few things. Before I give you a number, can you help me understand what problem you're trying to solve?" | Prevents premature price anchoring; re-anchors to value |
| "Can you send me some information?" | "Sure, I'll email our brochure" | "I'd be happy to. Just so I send you the most relevant information, what specifically are you looking to learn?" | Prevents the "send and disappear" pattern; qualifies the request |
| "How does your product compare to [competitor]?" | "We're better at X, Y, Z" | "Before I compare, I'm curious — what prompted you to ask about [competitor] specifically?" | Uncovers whether they're actively evaluating the competitor or just curious |
| "Do you have a feature that does X?" | "Yes, we have that" | "That's an interesting question. Why is that important to you?" | Uncovers the need behind the feature request |
| "When can we get started?" | "We can have you live in 2 weeks" | "That's great to hear. Before we talk timeline, let me make sure we're aligned on exactly what you need. What does 'getting started' look like for you?" | Prevents scope mismatches; confirms understanding |

**The Reversing Rule**: Reverse on 60-70% of prospect questions. Not every question — that becomes annoying. But reverse on any question that involves price, features, competitors, or timeline. These questions always have a deeper intent.

### Negative Reverse Selling

**Definition**: Taking the prospect's statement and pushing it in the opposite (negative) direction to test their commitment and uncover the truth.

**Why It Works**: Buyers often say positive things they don't mean ("We're very interested," "This looks great") to be polite or to get the seller to lower their guard. Negative reverse selling tests whether the positive statement is genuine.

**Negative Reverse Patterns:**

| Prospect Statement | Standard Response (Wrong) | Negative Reverse (Right) | Effect |
|-------------------|--------------------------|-------------------------|--------|
| "This looks really interesting." | "Great! Let me show you more..." | "Interesting? That's a pretty lukewarm response. Is this not a priority for you?" | Forces the prospect to clarify their level of interest |
| "We might be interested." | "Wonderful! When would you like to do a demo?" | "Might be? Sounds like you're not really sure this is relevant. Should we stop here?" | Tests whether the interest is real or polite |
| "The price seems reasonable." | "Great, so we're aligned on pricing." | "Reasonable? That's not exactly a ringing endorsement. Is the value not clear?" | Surfaces value objections that would emerge later |
| "I need to talk to my team." | "Of course, take your time." | "That's usually what people say when they're not convinced. Is there something specific you're not sure about?" | Cuts through the polite deflection to the real objection |
| "We're definitely going to move forward." | "Excellent! Let me get the contract ready." | "That's a strong statement. What could change between now and signing?" | Surfaces risks that could derail the deal at the last minute |

**The Negative Reverse Rule**: Use negative reverse selling when you sense the prospect is being polite rather than genuine. The pattern is: positive statement → push negative → observe whether they fight for the positive (genuine interest) or retreat (polite disinterest).

### The Stripping Line Technique

**Definition**: Repeatedly asking "and then what?" to uncover the full chain of consequences or decisions that follow from the prospect's statement. The stripping line reveals what the prospect doesn't want to think about — the downstream impact they're avoiding.

**Stripping Line Example:**

| Step | Question | Prospect Response |
|------|----------|------------------|
| 1 | "What happens after you complete your current project?" | "We'll evaluate new tools" |
| 2 | "And then what?" | "We'll select a vendor and negotiate" |
| 3 | "And then what?" | "We'll implement — probably takes 3 months" |
| 4 | "And then what?" | "We'll start seeing results — maybe Q3" |
| 5 | "And then what?" | "...We'll have lost another 6 months of pipeline productivity" |

The stripping line works because the prospect starts with a comfortable narrative ("we'll evaluate, select, implement") and each "and then what?" pushes them closer to the uncomfortable truth they've been avoiding.

## Sandler in Cold Outreach

### The Sandler Cold Email Framework

Traditional cold emails push for a meeting. Sandler cold emails invite evaluation and create a "no-pressure" dynamic.

**Template Structure:**

| Component | Purpose | Example |
|-----------|---------|---------|
| Pattern Interrupt | Break the expected sales email pattern | "I'm not sure if what we do is right for you." |
| Pain Hypothesis | State a problem you suspect they have | "Most [role]s in [industry] tell us their SDRs spend 60%+ of their time on research instead of selling." |
| Qualification Frame | Position as mutual evaluation, not a pitch | "If that's not a problem for you, no need to respond. But if it is, I have an idea that might help." |
| Low-Commitment CTA | Offer a low-risk next step | "Worth a 15-minute conversation? If not, that's fine too." |

**Sandler Cold Email Examples:**

**Example 1 — The Honest Approach:**
> Subject: Not sure if this is relevant
>
> Hi [Name],
>
> I'll be honest — I don't know if what we do is right for [Company]. We help SDR teams automate prospect research, which typically frees up 15-20 hours per rep per week. If your team doesn't have that problem, we're probably not a fit.
>
> But if research time is eating into your reps' selling time, I have an idea that might help. Worth 15 minutes to explore? If not, no worries.
>
> [Signature]

**Example 2 — The Negative Reverse:**
> Subject: Probably not a priority right now
>
> Hi [Name],
>
> Most SDR leaders I talk to say manual research is their biggest time sink — but they've learned to live with it. If that's you, this email isn't relevant.
>
> If, on the other hand, you're tired of accepting 60% research time as "just how it works," I'd like to share what's working for teams that have automated that process. 15 minutes — and I'll tell you upfront if I think we can help or not.
>
> [Signature]

**Example 3 — The Stripping Line:**
> Subject: What happens after you hire those 3 SDRs?
>
> Hi [Name],
>
> I saw [Company] is hiring SDRs. Quick question: what happens after they're hired?
>
> Most teams find that new SDRs spend their first 2-3 months learning how to research prospects — which means they're not generating pipeline for a full quarter. Teams that automate the research process ramp new reps in weeks instead of months.
>
> If faster ramp time would change your hiring ROI calculus, worth a 15-minute conversation?
>
> [Signature]

### Sandler Qualification for AI Agents

The Judge agent implements Sandler qualification gates at each pipeline stage:

```typescript
interface SandlerQualification {
  deal_id: string;
  
  bonding_rapport: {
    score: number;           // 0-100
    evidence: string;
    defense_shield_lowered: boolean;   // Has the prospect dropped their guard?
    equal_business_stance: boolean;    // Are we positioned as peers?
  };
  
  upfront_contract: {
    established: boolean;
    purpose_defined: boolean;
    time_confirmed: boolean;
    agenda_set: boolean;
    outcome_defined: boolean;
    exit_clause_given: boolean;
  };
  
  pain_funnel: {
    surface_complaint: string | null;
    symptoms: string[];
    root_cause: string | null;
    personal_impact: string | null;
    quantified_pain: {
      amount: number | null;
      timeframe: string | null;
    };
    commitment_to_solve: number;    // 1-10
    depth_level: 1-8;               // How deep in the funnel we've gone
  };
  
  budget: {
    budget_exists: boolean | null;
    budget_range: string | null;
    budget_authority: 'has_it' | 'needs_approval' | 'unknown';
    investment_receptivity: number;  // 1-10
  };
  
  decision: {
    decision_maker_identified: boolean;
    decision_process_mapped: boolean;
    decision_criteria_documented: boolean;
    decision_timeline_known: boolean;
    veto_risk_identified: boolean;
  };
  
  stage_status: 'bonding' | 'upfront_contract' | 'pain' | 'budget' | 'decision' | 'fulfillment' | 'post_sell';
  stage_sealed: boolean;            // Can we advance to the next stage?
  blocking_issues: string[];
}
```

**Stage Gate Rules:**

| From → To | Must Be Sealed | Disqualify If |
|-----------|---------------|---------------|
| Bonding → Up-Front Contract | Prospect is engaged and honest | Prospect is disengaged or clearly not interested |
| Up-Front Contract → Pain | Both parties agree on conversation purpose and ground rules | Prospect refuses to agree to ground rules |
| Pain → Budget | Pain is quantified and commitment to solve is ≥ 7/10 | Pain is unquantified or commitment is < 5/10 |
| Budget → Decision | Budget exists or is obtainable; investment range is feasible | No budget and no path to budget |
| Decision → Fulfillment | Decision process is fully mapped; all stakeholders identified | Decision process is unknown or impossibly complex |
| Fulfillment → Post-Sell | Solution presented matches stated pain; budget confirmed | Prospect wants features unrelated to their stated pain |

## Sandler vs. Traditional Selling — Key Differences

| Dimension | Traditional Selling | Sandler Selling |
|-----------|-------------------|-----------------|
| Philosophy | Persuade the buyer to buy | Help the buyer discover they need to buy |
| Seller posture | Subordinate (seeking approval) | Equal (evaluating fit) |
| Question approach | Questions to gather info for pitch | Questions to help prospect self-discover |
| Objection handling | Overcome objections | Prevent objections through qualification |
| Price conversation | After the demo, respond to price concerns | Before the demo, confirm budget willingness |
| Presentation | Full product demo | Focused on stated pain only |
| Closing | Push for the close | Let the prospect close themselves |
| Unqualified deals | "Keep them warm, they might buy" | Disqualify fast; invest time in qualified deals |
| Success measure | Activity volume (calls, demos, proposals) | Qualification quality (pain depth, budget confirmation) |

## Best Practices & Anti-Patterns

### Best Practices

1. **Seal every stage before advancing.** The Sandler submarine metaphor is not optional. If Pain isn't quantified, don't discuss Budget. If Budget isn't confirmed, don't present. Shortcutting stages is how deals stall and pipeline gets polluted.

2. **Use the up-front contract in every conversation — including internal ones.** UFC isn't just for prospect calls. Use it in team meetings, manager 1:1s, and cross-functional conversations. It creates clarity everywhere.

3. **Disqualify early and often.** A qualified "no" is more valuable than an unqualified "maybe." Every moment spent on an unqualified deal is stolen from a qualified one.

4. **Let the prospect do the selling.** Your job is to ask questions, not to pitch. If you're talking more than 30% of the time in a discovery call, you're selling, not qualifying.

5. **Reverse on every critical question.** When the prospect asks about price, features, or competitors — reverse. The question behind the question is always more important than the question itself.

6. **Use negative reverse to test commitment.** Don't accept "we're interested" at face value. Push negative: "Interested? That's not exactly a commitment. Are you really going to prioritize this?" If they fight for it, it's real. If they back off, it's polite.

7. **Present only to stated pain.** The demo is not a product tour — it's a surgical demonstration of how you solve their specific, quantified problem. Every feature you show that doesn't relate to their pain is a distraction and a potential objection.

8. **Always give the prospect an out.** "If this isn't right, tell me. I'd rather know now than waste both our time." This sounds counter-intuitive, but it builds trust and actually increases close rates.

### Anti-Patterns

1. **The Free Consulting Trap.** Answering every prospect question thoroughly without reversing or qualifying. This makes you a consultant, not a salesperson — and consultants don't get paid.

2. **The Premature Pitch.** Presenting your solution before Pain, Budget, and Decision are sealed. This is the most common Sandler violation and the #1 cause of "we need to think about it" responses.

3. **The Polite Accommodation.** Accepting every "we're interested" at face value without testing commitment. Sandler teaches you to be skeptical of positive signals and curious about them.

4. **Skipping the Up-Front Contract.** Jumping straight into discovery without establishing ground rules. This leads to misaligned expectations, wasted calls, and prospects who feel ambushed.

5. **The Budget Avoidance.** Being afraid to discuss budget because "it might scare them off." If budget is a deal-killer, you want to know that before investing weeks in the opportunity.

6. **Over-Reversing.** Reversing on every single question, including simple ones. "How's the weather?" → "Why do you ask?" This is annoying, not Sandler. Reverse on strategic questions; answer straightforward ones.

7. **The Desperate Close.** Pushing for the close instead of letting the prospect close themselves. If you've done the qualification correctly, the prospect should be asking "how do we get started?" — not the other way around.
