### Background

LearnPlatform built a learning analytics platform that helped universities measure student engagement, identify at-risk learners, and improve educational outcomes through data-driven instruction. The platform integrated with any LMS (Canvas, Blackboard, D2L Brightspace) and provided faculty with actionable dashboards without requiring data science expertise. The company had achieved strong results with 15 early-adopter institutions, demonstrating 12% improvement in course completion rates and 8% improvement in student retention.

However, scaling beyond early adopters proved exceptionally difficult. University procurement was unlike any other B2B sales cycle—it combined the complexity of enterprise sales (multiple stakeholders, long timelines, committee decisions) with the idiosyncrasies of academic governance (shared governance, faculty senate input, accreditation alignment, budget cycle dependency). The total addressable market of 4,000+ degree-granting institutions in the US was fragmented across segments with very different needs: R1 research universities, regional comprehensive universities, liberal arts colleges, community colleges, and HBCUs each required distinct value propositions and different committee structures.

LearnPlatform's two SDRs were spending most of their time on manual research—finding the right contacts at each university, understanding the institution's strategic priorities, and identifying the right timing based on budget cycles and academic calendars. The average SDR made 200+ calls per month but booked only 2 meetings, a 1% conversion rate that was unsustainable.

## 2. Challenge

LearnPlatform helped universities measure and improve learning outcomes but faced unique EdTech sales challenges:

- **Extended procurement cycles**: University purchasing required committee approval, budget cycles, and academic calendar alignment. The typical procurement path involved: faculty interest → department chair approval → IT review → provost budget allocation → procurement office processing → faculty senate IT committee review → final contract. This process took 12-18 months and could be derailed at any stage by any committee member.

- **Complex governance**: Department chairs, IT, Provost, procurement, faculty senate all had input. Unlike corporate buyers where a C-suite executive can authorize purchase, universities operate under "shared governance" where faculty have significant decision-making authority. A single influential faculty member on the IT committee could block a purchase even with Provost approval.

- **Seasonal buying patterns**: Budget decisions made in spring, purchases in summer/fall. The academic calendar created narrow windows of opportunity: February-April for budget planning, May-June for procurement, July-August for implementation, September for go-live. Outreach in October-January had minimal conversion because budgets were already allocated.

- **Limited IT budget**: Competing priorities with LMS, SIS, and infrastructure needs. University IT budgets are typically 3-5% of total operating budget, compared to 5-8% in corporate environments. Learning analytics competed against mandatory infrastructure upgrades, LMS renewals, and cybersecurity investments.

- **Risk aversion**: Universities slow to adopt new technology; require extensive proof and references. The "nobody gets fired for buying IBM" mentality was amplified in academia, where faculty tenure created particularly long institutional memory of failed technology implementations. References from peer institutions were the most powerful trust signal.

**Faculty Champion Identification and Engagement:**

One of the most powerful tactics was identifying faculty at target institutions who had published research on assessment, learning analytics, or student success. Bard crafted personalized outreach referencing their specific publications:

### Phase 5: Committee-Aware Pipeline (Weeks 5-52)

Flow tracked multi-stakeholder engagement with committee-specific progression rules:

**Committee-Aware Pipeline Stages:**

| Stage | Stakeholder Focus | Key Actions | Exit Criteria |
|-------|-------------------|-------------|---------------|
| 1. Champion Identified | Faculty or Assessment Director | Research sharing, peer case studies, faculty publication engagement | Faculty member expresses interest |
| 2. IT Approval | CIO / IT Director | Security review, integration demo, FERPA documentation | IT sign-off obtained |
| 3. Academic Alignment | Provost / VP Academic | Strategic plan alignment presentation, ROI model, retention impact analysis | Provost budget allocation |
| 4. Faculty Committee | Faculty Senate IT Committee | Committee presentation, pilot results, faculty reference | Committee approval vote |
| 5. Procurement | Dir. Procurement | E&I pricing, standard higher-ed contract terms, implementation timeline | Contract executed |
| 6. Implementation | All | Summer implementation, fall go-live, faculty training | Live before semester start |

**Pipeline Progression Rules:**
- A deal cannot advance past Stage 2 without IT security approval (prevents security review delays later)
- A deal cannot advance past Stage 3 without Provost engagement (prevents deals dying from lack of budget)
- Faculty committee presentation automatically triggered at Stage 4 (ensures governance compliance)
- Implementation scheduling must align with academic calendar (summer preferred for minimal disruption)

## 6. Key Decision Points

1. **Week 2: Target Segment Priority** — Debate between targeting R1 universities (prestigious but slow) vs. community colleges (faster but smaller deals). Decision: prioritize community colleges and online-first universities for initial traction, build R1 pipeline for long-term.
2. **Week 4: Academic Tone Calibration** — SDRs initially used standard B2B tone. First 50 outreach attempts generated zero replies. Decision: rewrite all sequences in academic-appropriate language (evidence-based, peer-reviewed, non-commercial).
3. **Week 6: Faculty Champion Strategy** — VP Sales initially resisted "going around" the CIO to faculty. Data from early adopters showed faculty-initiated deals closed 4x faster. Decision: adopt dual-track approach (CIO + Faculty champion simultaneously).
4. **Week 10: Pilot vs. Direct Sale** — Debate on whether to offer free pilots or require paid commitments. Decision: offer semester-aligned paid pilot ($5K) with defined success metrics and automatic conversion to annual contract if metrics met.

## 8. Lessons Learned

1. **Academic calendar is everything**: Outreach timed to budget planning cycles (Feb-Apr) had 3x higher engagement than summer outreach. The academic calendar is non-negotiable—there are no "accelerated" procurement cycles in higher education. Alignment, not urgency, wins.

2. **Faculty champions are the key**: Deals with an internal faculty advocate closed 4x faster than those without. Faculty have credibility with other faculty that no vendor can replicate. The most effective approach was identifying faculty who had published on assessment or learning analytics and engaging them as research partners, not sales targets.

3. **Evidence beats features**: Academic buyers responded to peer-reviewed evidence and case studies, not feature lists. A single data point ("12% improvement in course completion at a peer institution") was more compelling than a complete feature comparison. Academic culture values evidence over assertion.

4. **Pilot programs reduce risk**: Offering semester-aligned pilots addressed the "try before you buy" mentality. The key was structuring the pilot as a research study with defined methodology and success metrics—this language resonated with academic buyers in a way that "free trial" did not.

5. **Strategic plan language is a shortcut**: Referencing the institution's own stated goals in outreach demonstrated alignment and earned responses. When LearnPlatform could say "your 2025-2030 strategic plan identifies 'data-driven student success' as Priority 3—here's how we help you achieve that," Provosts responded.

6. **Peer institution comparisons create urgency**: Showing that similar institutions had already adopted learning analytics—and were achieving better outcomes—created both competitive pressure and safety-in-numbers reassurance. "3 of your 5 peer comparables are using LearnPlatform" was the most effective urgency messaging.

7. **FERPA compliance is the security review**: Unlike corporate security reviews that focus on SOC 2 and ISO 27001, higher education compliance centers on FERPA (Family Educational Rights and Privacy Act). Proactively providing FERPA compliance documentation eliminated 80% of IT objections.
