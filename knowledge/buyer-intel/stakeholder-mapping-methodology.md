# Stakeholder Mapping Methodology

Stakeholder mapping is the systematic process of identifying all individuals who influence or participate in a purchase decision, understanding their roles and motivations, and developing engagement strategies for each. The most effective framework combines power/interest grid analysis (who has decision power vs. who has influence) with relationship mapping (how stakeholders relate to each other). The power/interest grid places stakeholders in four quadrants: high power/high interest (key players — manage closely), high power/low interest (keep satisfied — don't overwhelm but ensure support), low power/high interest (keep informed — they can be champions or internal marketers), and low power/low interest (monitor — minimal engagement needed). Relationship mapping adds a second dimension: understanding reporting structures, peer relationships, and informal influence networks. This reveals: who influences the economic buyer, which stakeholders might block the deal, and which relationships can be leveraged for warm introductions. LeadReach agents should automate initial stakeholder mapping by: extracting participants from email threads and meeting invites, enriching contacts with title and role data from LinkedIn, identifying missing stakeholders by comparing the current map against typical buying committee patterns, and recommending engagement strategies for each stakeholder based on their quadrant position.

## The Strategic Importance of Stakeholder Mapping

### Why Most Deals Fail at the Stakeholder Level

Research consistently shows that the primary cause of B2B deal loss is not product deficiency or pricing misalignment — it's stakeholder mismanagement. According to Gartner, the average B2B purchase involves 6-10 stakeholders, and the most common reason deals stall is that one or more key stakeholders were not engaged during the sales process. Specifically:

- 65% of lost deals involve an unknown stakeholder who influenced the decision from outside the seller's awareness
- 40% of stalled deals are stuck because the economic buyer was never directly engaged
- 35% of lost deals fail because a blocker was not identified and addressed early enough
- 50% of deals that close but later churn involve stakeholders who were not consulted during the purchase

These statistics underscore a fundamental truth: you cannot win a deal if you don't know who is making the decision. Stakeholder mapping is not an optional exercise — it is the foundation upon which all other sales activities are built. A perfectly crafted value proposition delivered to the wrong stakeholder, or delivered without awareness of other stakeholders who will be consulted, is wasted effort.

### The Cost of Incomplete Stakeholder Maps

| Mapping Gap | Risk | Probability | Impact | Mitigation |
|------------|------|------------|--------|-----------|
| Unknown economic buyer | Budget access blocked | 30% | Deal loss | Early identification through org chart research |
| Unknown technical buyer | Technical veto at late stage | 25% | Deal delay or loss | Proactive technical stakeholder identification |
| Unknown blocker | Deal killed from within | 20% | Deal loss | Identify potential blockers early, address concerns |
| Unknown influencer | Decision goes to competitor | 15% | Deal loss | Map influence networks beyond formal hierarchy |
| Unknown end users | Adoption failure post-sale | 10% | Churn risk | Include end users in evaluation process |

## The Stakeholder Mapping Framework

### Framework Overview: The Five-Layer Model

LeadReach's stakeholder mapping methodology uses a five-layer model that progressively deepens the understanding of each stakeholder's role, influence, relationships, and engagement strategy:

| Layer | Focus | Key Questions | Output |
|-------|-------|--------------|--------|
| **Layer 1: Identification** | Who are the stakeholders? | Who is involved in or affected by this decision? | Complete stakeholder list |
| **Layer 2: Classification** | What roles do they play? | What is each stakeholder's committee role and power level? | Role assignments and power/interest grid |
| **Layer 3: Motivation** | Why do they care? | What are each stakeholder's goals, concerns, and decision criteria? | Motivation profiles for each stakeholder |
| **Layer 4: Relationships** | How are they connected? | How do stakeholders relate to and influence each other? | Relationship map and influence network |
| **Layer 5: Strategy** | How do we engage them? | What is the optimal engagement approach for each stakeholder? | Stakeholder engagement plan |

### Layer 1: Stakeholder Identification

The first layer focuses on comprehensively identifying every individual who may influence the purchase decision. This is the most critical layer because unidentified stakeholders are the primary cause of deal loss.

**Identification Methods**:

| Method | Data Source | Stakeholders Revealed | LeadReach Agent |
|--------|-----------|----------------------|-----------------|
| CRM analysis | Existing contact records, opportunity team | Known contacts with engagement history | Scout enriches existing contacts with updated titles and roles |
| Email thread analysis | Email participants on deal-related correspondence | Active participants in the buying conversation | Scout extracts and analyzes email thread participants |
| Meeting analysis | Calendar invites, meeting attendees | Stakeholders who are attending evaluation meetings | Scout tracks meeting participant patterns over time |
| LinkedIn mapping | Organizational chart, connections | Stakeholders in relevant departments and reporting lines | Scout maps organizational structure from LinkedIn data |
| Champion input | Champion's knowledge of internal process | Insider perspective on decision-making process | Forge generates discovery questions to surface unknown stakeholders |
| Job posting analysis | Open positions at the target account | Future stakeholders and signals of organizational needs | Scout monitors job postings for relevant role signals |
| Press release analysis | Company announcements, leadership changes | New executives and organizational changes | Scout tracks trigger events that affect stakeholder composition |
| Industry pattern matching | Typical buying committee for deal type | Missing stakeholders based on industry benchmarks | Judge compares current map against ideal committee patterns |

**The Stakeholder Identification Checklist**:

For every deal, systematically check the following stakeholder categories:

1. **Budget holders**: Who controls the budget that would fund this purchase? (Economic buyer + their finance partner)
2. **Decision makers**: Who has authority to approve or reject the purchase? (Often multiple levels of approval)
3. **Technical evaluators**: Who will assess technical fit and integration requirements? (Engineering, IT, architecture)
4. **Security reviewers**: Who will conduct the security and compliance assessment? (CISO, security team, GRC)
5. **End users**: Who will use the product daily? (ICs, team leads, managers)
6. **Business sponsors**: Who is advocating for the purchase? (Champion + their allies)
7. **Potential blockers**: Who might oppose the purchase and why? (Competing priorities, existing vendor loyalists)
8. **Procurement contacts**: Who manages the procurement process? (Procurement manager, legal)
9. **Executive sponsors**: Which C-suite executives will review or approve? (CEO, CFO, CTO)
10. **External influencers**: Who outside the organization might influence the decision? (Analysts, consultants, board members)

### Layer 2: Stakeholder Classification

The second layer assigns each identified stakeholder to a committee role and positions them on the power/interest grid. This classification drives the engagement strategy for each stakeholder.

**The Power/Interest Grid**

The power/interest grid is the foundational tool for stakeholder classification:

```
                    HIGH INTEREST
                         |
    Keep Informed        |        Manage Closely
    (Champions,          |        (Economic buyers,
    End users,           |        Key influencers,
    Internal advocates)  |        Active champions)
                         |
 LOW POWER ──────────────┼────────────────────── HIGH POWER
                         |
    Monitor              |        Keep Satisfied
    (Peripheral          |        (Senior executives,
    stakeholders,        |        Board members,
    Uninvolved depts)    |        External influencers)
                         |
                    LOW INTEREST
```

**Quadrant-Specific Engagement Strategies**:

| Quadrant | Stakeholder Type | Engagement Strategy | Communication Frequency | Content Type |
|----------|-----------------|-------------------|------------------------|-------------|
| **Manage Closely** (High Power / High Interest) | Economic buyer, key influencers, strong champion | Proactive, personalized engagement. Regular check-ins. Direct access to senior resources. | Weekly or bi-weekly | Customized business cases, executive briefings, peer references |
| **Keep Satisfied** (High Power / Low Interest) | Senior executives not directly involved, board members | Minimal but meaningful engagement. Ensure they have positive impression. Don't overwhelm. | Monthly or as needed | Executive summaries, strategic alignment documentation |
| **Keep Informed** (Low Power / High Interest) | End users, potential champions, internal advocates | Regular information sharing. Make them look good. Enable them to advocate internally. | Bi-weekly | Educational content, workflow documentation, team-level metrics |
| **Monitor** (Low Power / Low Interest) | Peripheral stakeholders, uninvolved departments | Minimal engagement. Watch for changes in their interest or power level. | Quarterly or less | General updates, newsletters |

**Committee Role Classification**:

Beyond the power/interest grid, each stakeholder should be classified into one or more committee roles:

| Role | Power Level | Interest Level | Primary Concern | Engagement Priority |
|------|-----------|---------------|----------------|-------------------|
| Economic buyer | Very High | Variable | ROI, budget impact | Critical — must be engaged before deal can close |
| Technical buyer | High | High | Architecture fit, integration | High — technical veto can kill deal |
| Champion | Variable | Very High | Solving their problem, career advancement | Critical — primary internal advocate |
| End user | Low | High | Usability, workflow improvement | Medium — adoption influence |
| Blocker | Variable | Variable | Competing priorities, existing relationships | High — must be identified and addressed |
| Influencer | Variable | Variable | Strategic alignment, peer validation | Medium — can tip the decision |
| Procurement | High | Medium | Process compliance, contract terms | Medium — engage when deal enters procurement |
| Security reviewer | High | Medium | Security posture, compliance | Medium — can create significant delays |

### Layer 3: Stakeholder Motivation Analysis

The third layer goes beyond role classification to understand each stakeholder's specific motivations, concerns, and decision criteria. This enables truly personalized engagement.

**Motivation Analysis Framework**:

| Motivation Dimension | Key Questions | Information Sources |
|---------------------|--------------|-------------------|
| **Professional goals** | What are they measured on? What does success look like for them? | Title analysis, KPI research, public statements |
| **Personal motivations** | What drives them personally? Career advancement, problem-solving, recognition? | Behavioral patterns, career trajectory, content engagement |
| **Organizational context** | What pressures are they under? What initiatives are they responsible for? | Company announcements, earnings calls, department priorities |
| **Pain points** | What challenges are they currently experiencing? What's preventing goal achievement? | Discovery conversations, content engagement patterns, team signals |
| **Risk tolerance** | How risk-averse are they? What would make them uncomfortable with a new solution? | Industry context, company stage, role requirements |
| **Decision criteria** | What specific criteria will they use to evaluate solutions? | Role-specific criteria, discovery questions, evaluation documents |
| **Information preferences** | How do they prefer to receive information? What sources do they trust? | Content engagement patterns, channel preferences, peer influence |

**Motivation Profiling by Committee Role**:

| Role | Primary Motivation | Secondary Motivation | Key Fear | Decision Driver |
|------|-------------------|---------------------|---------|----------------|
| Economic buyer | Financial ROI, strategic alignment | Risk management, board narrative | Wasting budget on failed investment | Quantified business case with peer validation |
| Technical buyer | Architecture integrity, team productivity | Technical innovation, career growth | Creating technical debt or integration nightmare | Technical validation through POC and architecture review |
| Champion | Solving their problem, career advancement | Team productivity, recognition | Wasting political capital on a failed initiative | Evidence that solution delivers on promises |
| End user | Easier workday, skill advancement | Job security, avoiding disruption | Learning curve, workflow disruption | Hands-on experience proving workflow improvement |
| Blocker | Protecting their priorities, existing investments | Organizational stability, risk avoidance | Being marginalized or overridden | Inclusion in process, respect for their concerns |
| Influencer | Providing valuable guidance, maintaining credibility | Staying informed, being consulted | Recommending a solution that fails | Thorough evaluation and peer validation |

### Layer 4: Relationship Mapping

The fourth layer maps the relationships between stakeholders, revealing the informal influence networks that often matter more than formal reporting structures.

**Relationship Types to Map**:

| Relationship Type | Description | How It Affects the Deal | Detection Method |
|------------------|------------|------------------------|-----------------|
| **Reporting relationship** | Formal authority structure (manager → director → VP) | Determines approval chain and veto power | LinkedIn org chart, company directory |
| **Peer relationship** | Colleagues at the same level who collaborate regularly | Cross-functional influence and information sharing | Meeting patterns, email analysis, champion input |
| **Mentor/mentee** | Senior person who advises and influences a junior person | Decision influence through trusted advice | Career history overlap, LinkedIn endorsements |
| **Alliance** | Stakeholders who share priorities and support each other | Coalition building for purchase support | Shared meeting attendance, aligned feedback |
| **Rivalry** | Stakeholders who compete for resources or influence | Potential blocking behavior or competing agendas | Opposing feedback, competing priorities |
| **Vendor relationship** | Stakeholder with connection to a competing vendor | Potential source of competitive intelligence or bias | Job history, LinkedIn connections, conference attendance |

**The Relationship Mapping Process**:

1. **Start with formal structure**: Map the org chart for relevant departments. This provides the foundation for understanding formal authority and approval chains.

2. **Add informal connections**: Layer on peer relationships, mentorship connections, and cross-functional collaborations. These often matter more than formal reporting lines.

3. **Identify influence patterns**: Determine who influences whom. The person who controls the decision isn't always the person with the highest title — it's often the person whose opinion the decision-maker trusts most.

4. **Map alliance and opposition patterns**: Identify stakeholders who are aligned (and will support each other) and those who are opposed (and may work against each other). These dynamics significantly affect committee behavior.

5. **Identify warm introduction paths**: Find relationships between your existing contacts and target stakeholders that can facilitate warm introductions.

**Relationship Visualization**:

Create a stakeholder relationship map that shows:
- Nodes: Each stakeholder, sized by power level and colored by interest level
- Solid lines: Reporting relationships (formal authority)
- Dashed lines: Influence relationships (informal power)
- Green connections: Supportive or allied relationships
- Red connections: Opposed or competitive relationships
- Blue connections: Neutral or unknown relationships
- Arrows: Direction of influence (A influences B)

The fifth layer combines insights from all previous layers to develop a comprehensive engagement strategy for each stakeholder.

## Stakeholder Mapping by Deal Stage

### Early Stage: Problem Recognition (1-3 Stakeholders)

At this stage, only 1-3 stakeholders are typically involved. The stakeholder map is small but the foundation is being laid for future committee expansion.

**Mapping Focus**:
- Identify the initial contact and their role in the organization
- Determine if they have the authority and influence to drive a purchase
- Assess whether they are a potential champion or just an interested party
- Begin mapping the organizational context around them (who they report to, who they work with)

**LeadReach Agent Actions**:
- **Scout**: Enriches the initial contact with title, tenure, and organizational context data
- **Judge**: Scores the contact as a potential champion and recommends whether to invest in the relationship
- **Forge**: Generates initial discovery questions designed to surface additional stakeholders

### Mid Stage: Solution Exploration (3-6 Stakeholders)

The committee is expanding as the initial contact involves colleagues in the evaluation. This is the critical stage for comprehensive stakeholder mapping.

**Mapping Focus**:
- Identify all stakeholders who have been pulled into the evaluation
- Classify each stakeholder by committee role and power/interest position
- Begin mapping relationships between stakeholders
- Identify gaps in the stakeholder map (missing economic buyer, missing technical buyer)
- Develop engagement strategies for each identified stakeholder

**LeadReach Agent Actions**:
- **Scout**: Monitors for new stakeholders from the same company engaging with content, identifies meeting participants, and maps LinkedIn connections
- **Judge**: Compares the current stakeholder map against the ideal committee for the deal type and flags gaps
- **Forge**: Generates stakeholder-specific outreach messages and recommends engagement approaches

### Late Stage: Evaluation & Decision (6-10+ Stakeholders)

The full committee is now engaged, and the stakeholder map should be comprehensive. Late-stage mapping focuses on understanding stakeholder positions and building consensus.

**Mapping Focus**:
- Verify the stakeholder map is complete (no unknown stakeholders)
- Understand each stakeholder's current position (supportive, neutral, opposed)
- Map the influence dynamics that will determine the decision
- Develop consensus-building strategies based on stakeholder positions
- Identify and address any remaining blockers

**LeadReach Agent Actions**:
- **Scout**: Continuously monitors for new stakeholders entering the process and alerts to any previously unidentified participants
- **Judge**: Tracks stakeholder position changes (moving from neutral to supportive, or from neutral to opposed) and recommends intervention strategies
- **Forge**: Generates consensus-building materials (stakeholder-specific messaging, internal advocacy templates)

## Advanced Stakeholder Mapping Techniques

### The Multi-Thread Mapping Strategy

Multi-threading is the practice of building relationships with multiple stakeholders simultaneously, rather than relying on a single thread of communication. Effective multi-threading requires comprehensive stakeholder mapping.

**Multi-Threading Rules**:

1. **Never rely on a single stakeholder**: Every deal should have at least 3-4 engaged stakeholders from different parts of the buying committee.

2. **Thread from champion outward**: Use the champion as the starting point, but build independent relationships with other stakeholders.

3. **Engage across committee roles**: Have active threads with the economic buyer, technical buyer, and at least one end user or influencer.

4. **Maintain thread independence**: Each stakeholder thread should be able to survive without the others. If the champion leaves, you should still have access to the economic buyer and technical evaluator.

5. **Coordinate messaging across threads**: Ensure that different stakeholders are hearing consistent but role-appropriate messaging from your team.

**Multi-Threading Engagement Matrix**:

| Stakeholder | Relationship Owner | Engagement Channel | Message Focus | Cadence |
|------------|-------------------|-------------------|--------------|---------|
| Economic buyer | AE / Executive sponsor | Direct email, exec meetings | Strategic value, ROI | Monthly |
| Technical buyer | SE / Solutions consultant | Technical deep-dives | Architecture, integration | Bi-weekly |
| Champion | AE / SDR | Regular calls, content sharing | Internal selling support | Weekly |
| End user | CSM / Product specialist | Trial support, training | Workflow improvement, UX | As needed |
| Procurement | Deal desk / Legal | Formal documentation | Contract terms, compliance | As deal requires |

### The Warm Introduction Mapping Strategy

Warm introductions convert at 3-5x the rate of cold outreach. Mapping potential warm introduction paths is a high-value stakeholder mapping activity.

**Introduction Path Types**:

| Path Type | Source | Conversion Rate | Effort Level |
|-----------|--------|----------------|-------------|
| Board connection | Board member at both companies | Very High | High — requires executive coordination |
| Investor connection | Shared VC or PE investor | Very High | Medium — investor can facilitate intro |
| Customer connection | Customer who recommends you to peer | Very High | Low — customer-initiated intro is gold |
| Former colleague | Someone who worked at both companies | High | Medium — requires LinkedIn research |
| Community connection | Shared professional community or association | Medium-High | Low — natural conversation starter |
| Event connection | Met at same conference or event | Medium | Low — event follow-up is expected |
| LinkedIn connection | 2nd-degree connection with mutual contact | Medium | Low — LinkedIn intro request |

**Introduction Mapping Process**:

1. **Identify target stakeholder**: Who do you need to reach that you don't have access to?
2. **Search your network**: Who in your organization (or customer base) has a connection to the target?
3. **Evaluate introduction quality**: Not all introductions are equal. Prioritize introductions from people the target stakeholder trusts and respects.
4. **Prepare the introducer**: Brief the person making the introduction on what you do, why the introduction is valuable for the target, and how to frame it.
5. **Follow up promptly**: When the introduction is made, respond within 24 hours with a personalized, value-first message.

### The Competitive Stakeholder Mapping Strategy

Competitive stakeholder mapping identifies which stakeholders in the buying committee have relationships with or preferences for competing vendors. This intelligence enables proactive competitive positioning.

**Competitive Signal Indicators**:

| Signal | What It Indicates | Detection Method |
|--------|------------------|-----------------|
| Stakeholder previously worked at competitor | Likely prefers competitor's approach | LinkedIn career history analysis |
| Stakeholder follows competitor on social media | Awareness and possible preference | Social media monitoring |
| Stakeholder speaks positively about competitor in meetings | Active preference or existing relationship | Meeting observation and notes |
| Competitor's customers attend same events | Potential competitive networking | Event attendee analysis |
| Job postings reference competitor's technology | Current or planned competitor usage | Job posting analysis |
| Company uses competitor's technology in adjacent area | Existing vendor relationship | Technographic data |

## Stakeholder Mapping for Different Organization Types

### Startup Stakeholder Mapping

Startups have small, fluid stakeholder maps. The founder/CEO is typically the primary decision-maker with direct involvement in all purchases.

**Typical Map**: Founder/CEO → Department lead → End user (2-4 stakeholders)

**Mapping Approach**: Focus on the founder/CEO and their direct reports. Identify the person closest to the problem. Map the founder's priorities and communication preferences.

**Key Insight**: Startup founders value speed and simplicity. Complex stakeholder mapping is unnecessary — focus on understanding the founder's priorities and the department lead's operational needs.

### Mid-Market Stakeholder Mapping

Mid-market companies have more structured decision processes with VP-level budget holders and Director-level evaluators. The stakeholder map is larger but still manageable.

**Typical Map**: VP → Director → Manager → End user + IT review (4-7 stakeholders)

**Mapping Approach**: Identify the VP budget holder, the Director who will manage the implementation, and the Manager who will oversee daily use. Map the IT review requirement and engage early.

**Key Insight**: Mid-market stakeholder maps often have a clear power structure. Map the VP's priorities and the Director's operational concerns. Don't overlook the IT review process, which is often the biggest source of delays.

### Enterprise Stakeholder Mapping

Enterprise organizations have complex stakeholder maps with multiple departments, formal evaluation processes, and extended approval chains. Comprehensive mapping is essential.

**Typical Map**: C-suite + VP (multiple) + Directors + Managers + IT + Security + Procurement + Legal (8-15+ stakeholders)

**Mapping Approach**: Systematic identification across all committee roles. Map formal and informal influence networks. Identify potential blockers and competitive stakeholders. Track engagement across all stakeholders simultaneously.

**Key Insight**: Enterprise stakeholder maps are dynamic — new stakeholders appear throughout the process. Continuously update the map and engage new stakeholders as they emerge. The most dangerous stakeholder is the one you don't know about.

### Government Stakeholder Mapping

Government organizations have the most formalized stakeholder structures with strict procurement regulations, multi-level approval requirements, and often public decision-making processes.

**Typical Map**: Program manager + Contracting officer + Technical evaluator + Security reviewer + Legal + End users (6-12 stakeholders)

**Mapping Approach**: Identify the contracting officer (they control the process), the program manager (they define the requirements), and the technical evaluator (they assess capability). Map the formal evaluation criteria and scoring process.

**Key Insight**: Government stakeholder maps are defined by the procurement process. Understanding the process is more important than mapping informal influence networks, which are less relevant in government contexts.

## Stakeholder Mapping Tools & Templates

### The Stakeholder Register

The stakeholder register is the master document that captures all stakeholder information in a structured format:

| Field | Description | Update Frequency |
|-------|------------|-----------------|
| Name | Full name of the stakeholder | As identified |
| Title | Current job title | Quarterly |
| Department | Functional department | As identified |
| Committee role | Economic buyer, technical buyer, champion, etc. | As roles become clear |
| Power level | High / Medium / Low | As influence becomes clear |
| Interest level | High / Medium / Low | As engagement becomes clear |
| Current position | Supportive / Neutral / Opposed / Unknown | Bi-weekly |
| Key concern | Primary concern about the purchase | As discovered |
| Decision criteria | How they evaluate solutions | As discovered |
| Influence network | Who they influence and who influences them | As discovered |
| Engagement owner | Who on the selling team owns this relationship | As assigned |
| Last engagement | Date and nature of last interaction | Each interaction |
| Next action | Planned next engagement step | Weekly |
| Risk assessment | Risk level and mitigation plan | As changes occur |

### The Power/Interest Grid Template

Create a visual power/interest grid for each active deal, positioning each stakeholder in the appropriate quadrant. Update the grid as stakeholder positions change.

### The Relationship Map Template

Create a visual relationship map showing all stakeholders and their connections. Use color coding and line styles to indicate the nature and strength of each relationship.

### The Engagement Plan Template

Create a consolidated engagement plan that lists each stakeholder, their engagement strategy, responsible team member, and planned next actions. Review and update weekly.

## LeadReach Agent Application: Stakeholder Mapping Intelligence

### Automated Stakeholder Identification

- **Scout Agent** automatically identifies stakeholders through multiple data sources: CRM contact enrichment, LinkedIn organizational mapping, email thread participant analysis, meeting attendee tracking, and job posting monitoring. It maintains a continuously updated stakeholder register for each active deal.

- **Judge Agent** compares the current stakeholder map against typical buying committee patterns for the deal type and industry, flagging gaps where key committee roles are unfilled (no economic buyer, no technical buyer, no security reviewer).

### Dynamic Stakeholder Classification

- **Judge Agent** automatically classifies stakeholders into committee roles based on title analysis, organizational position, and engagement behavior patterns. It assigns power and interest levels based on demonstrated influence and engagement depth.

- **Scout Agent** monitors for changes in stakeholder positions — promotions, departures, role changes — and updates the classification accordingly. It alerts the seller when a key stakeholder's position changes in ways that could affect the deal.

### Relationship Network Analysis

- **Scout Agent** maps stakeholder relationships by analyzing LinkedIn connections, shared meeting attendance, email thread participation patterns, and organizational chart data. It identifies warm introduction paths between the seller's network and target stakeholders.

- **Judge Agent** identifies influence patterns by analyzing how stakeholders interact in meetings and communications. It maps which stakeholders' opinions carry the most weight and recommends engagement strategies based on influence dynamics.

### Gap Detection & Multi-Threading Recommendations

- **Judge Agent** continuously analyzes the stakeholder map for gaps and risks:
  - **Single point of failure**: Only one stakeholder engaged in a critical committee role → recommend multi-threading
  - **Missing economic buyer**: No budget-holder identified → recommend champion-assisted introduction
  - **Unidentified stakeholders**: Stakeholder map is incomplete relative to deal complexity → recommend discovery questions
  - **Competitive stakeholders**: Stakeholders with competitor relationships → recommend competitive positioning

- **Forge Agent** generates stakeholder-specific engagement strategies based on the complete mapping analysis, including recommended messaging, content, channels, and cadence for each stakeholder based on their quadrant position, motivation profile, and relationship to other stakeholders.
