# Buying Committee Dynamics

The average B2B purchase involves 6-10 stakeholders in the buying committee, up from 5-6 a decade ago. Understanding committee dynamics is essential for multi-threaded selling strategies. The buying committee typically includes: an economic buyer (controls budget, usually VP+), a technical buyer (evaluates fit with existing architecture, usually Director of Engineering/IT), an end user (daily user of the product, usually IC or team lead), a champion (internal advocate, as described above), and a blocker (someone who opposes the purchase, often due to competing priorities, existing vendor relationships, or perceived risk). Key dynamics to understand include: the formal decision process (who approves, who signs, who vets), the informal influence network (who influences whom, whose opinion carries weight), the competing priorities within the committee (different stakeholders evaluate on different criteria), and the timeline pressures (external deadlines, budget cycles, contractual obligations). Mapping the buying committee early — identifying all stakeholders, their roles, their criteria, and their relationships — increases win rates by 30-40% compared to single-threaded approaches. LeadReach agents should automatically map buying committees by: analyzing email thread participants, identifying additional stakeholders from LinkedIn research, and flagging gaps (no economic buyer engaged, no technical buyer consulted) that create deal risk.

## The Evolution of Buying Committees

### Historical Context and Current State

The B2B buying committee has undergone a dramatic transformation over the past decade. In 2010, the average B2B purchase involved 5.4 stakeholders. By 2020, that number had grown to 6.8. Current research from Gartner and Forrester indicates the average enterprise purchase now involves 7-10 stakeholders, with some complex purchases involving 15+ individuals across multiple departments. This expansion reflects several fundamental shifts in how organizations make technology decisions.

The democratization of information has empowered individual contributors and middle managers to research and evaluate solutions independently, often before any formal buying process begins. Research indicates that B2B buyers complete 57-70% of their research before engaging with a vendor for the first time. This means that by the time a seller enters the picture, multiple stakeholders may already have formed opinions about the solution category.

The rise of cross-functional technology has expanded the number of departments affected by each purchase. A CRM implementation, for example, touches sales, marketing, customer success, operations, finance, and IT — each of which has a stakeholder who wants input into the decision. Similarly, a security tool purchase affects IT, compliance, legal, and every department that handles sensitive data.

Risk aversion in the post-pandemic era has led to more conservative decision-making processes. Organizations are requiring broader consensus before making technology investments, particularly for purchases that involve data migration, workflow changes, or significant budget commitments. The "sign-off chain" has grown longer, with more individuals required to approve purchases before contracts can be signed.

### Committee Size by Deal Type

| Deal Type | Typical Committee Size | Key Stakeholders | Decision Complexity |
|-----------|----------------------|-----------------|-------------------|
| Point solution (<$25K) | 2-3 | Manager + Director + IT review | Low |
| Department tool ($25K-$100K) | 4-6 | VP + Director + Team lead + IT + Procurement | Medium |

| Transformation initiative ($2M+) | 15-25+ | Board-level visibility + Full C-suite + Multiple VPs + IT + Security + External consultants | Extreme |

## Buying Committee Roles: Detailed Taxonomy

### The Economic Buyer

The economic buyer is the individual who controls the budget and has final authority to approve the purchase. In most organizations, this is a VP or C-suite executive. The economic buyer's primary concern is financial: does the investment deliver sufficient ROI, and is the budget available?

**Identifying the Economic Buyer**:
- Typically the VP or executive whose department budget funds the purchase
- May be the CRO for sales tools, CMO for marketing tools, CTO for engineering tools
- In some organizations, the CFO serves as the universal economic buyer for purchases above a threshold
- Look for: titles containing "VP", "Chief", or "Head of" in the relevant department

**Engaging the Economic Buyer**:
- Always provide quantified ROI projections with clear assumptions
- Reference peer company financial outcomes
- Offer flexible pricing and payment structures
- Provide executive-level materials (1-page summaries, ROI models)
- Engage through the champion — don't bypass to reach the economic buyer directly

**Economic Buyer Risk Patterns**:
- **Ghost economic buyer**: The champion can't get the economic buyer to engage — deal stalls indefinitely
- **Surprise economic buyer**: A budget-holder unknown to the seller appears at the final approval stage and raises new objections
- **Economic buyer turnover**: The VP who approved the budget leaves the company mid-deal

### The Technical Buyer

The technical buyer evaluates the solution's fit with existing architecture, integration requirements, and technical standards. This role is typically filled by a Director of Engineering, IT Manager, or Solutions Architect. The technical buyer has significant veto power — if they determine the solution is architecturally incompatible, the deal is dead regardless of business case strength.

**Identifying the Technical Buyer**:
- Director/Manager of Engineering, IT, or Architecture
- May be a Senior Engineer or Solutions Architect for technical purchases
- Often the person who asks the most detailed technical questions during demos
- Look for: titles containing "Engineering", "IT", "Architecture", "Infrastructure"

**Engaging the Technical Buyer**:
- Provide detailed technical documentation and API references
- Offer architecture review sessions with solution engineers
- Be transparent about limitations and integration requirements
- Address security and compliance proactively
- Offer proof-of-concept with defined technical success criteria

### The End User

The end user is the individual or team who will use the product daily. Their primary concern is usability — does this tool make my job easier or harder? End users have influence through adoption: even if the purchase is approved, if end users don't adopt the tool, the investment becomes shelfware and the champion looks bad.

**Identifying End Users**:
- IC-level contributors who work in the affected workflow
- Team leads and senior individual contributors
- Often the first people the champion consults informally
- Look for: titles matching the functional role the solution serves

**Engaging End Users**:
- Demonstrate workflow improvement and time savings
- Provide hands-on trial access
- Show UX that reduces friction vs. current tools
- Gather and amplify end user feedback to the champion
- Offer training and onboarding support

### The Champion

The champion is the internal advocate who actively promotes the solution. See the Champion Enablement Guide for detailed champion identification, enablement, and management strategies. In the context of buying committee dynamics, the champion plays a unique role as the bridge between the selling team and the buying organization.

**Champion's Role in Committee Dynamics**:
- Provides insider intelligence on stakeholder priorities and relationships
- Navigates organizational politics and identifies potential blockers
- Coordinates internal selling by presenting to different stakeholders
- Serves as the primary communication channel between seller and buying team
- Translates the vendor's value proposition into language that resonates internally

### The Blocker

The blocker is a stakeholder who actively opposes the purchase. Blockers are often the most underestimated committee role, as sellers tend to focus on champions and economic buyers while ignoring potential opposition. Identifying and addressing blockers early is essential — a blocker who surfaces late in the process can kill a deal that seemed destined to close.

**Blocker Archetypes**:

| Blocker Type | Motivation | Warning Signs | Strategy |
|-------------|-----------|--------------|----------|
| **Budget protector** | Competing priorities for limited budget | "We don't have budget for this", "This isn't a priority right now" | Demonstrate clear ROI and opportunity cost of inaction |
| **Incumbent loyalist** | Existing vendor relationship or personal investment | "We already use [competitor]", "[Competitor] is working fine" | Acknowledge value of current solution, highlight specific gaps |
| **Change resister** | Fear of disruption, learning curve, or workflow changes | "My team doesn't have time for this", "This will disrupt our process" | Emphasize change management support and phased rollout |
| **Power player** | Opposes because they weren't consulted or because supporting it would reduce their influence | Silence, passive resistance, or raising objections in group settings | Engage them directly, seek their input, address their concerns |
| **Technical skeptic** | Genuine technical concerns about architecture, security, or integration | "This doesn't fit our architecture", "I have security concerns" | Address concerns directly with technical evidence and expert support |
| **NIH syndrome** | "Not Invented Here" — prefers internal solutions | "We could build this ourselves", "Our team can handle this" | Provide build vs. buy analysis with time and cost comparisons |

**Blocker Engagement Strategy**:

1. **Identify early**: Look for stakeholders who should be involved but haven't been consulted, and stakeholders who have expressed reservations in meetings or communications.

2. **Understand the motivation**: Blockers oppose purchases for rational reasons (legitimate concerns) or political reasons (organizational dynamics). The engagement strategy depends on the motivation.

3. **Address legitimate concerns**: If the blocker has valid technical, financial, or operational concerns, address them directly with evidence and support. Don't dismiss or minimize their concerns.

4. **Neutralize political opposition**: If the blocker's opposition is political, find ways to include them in the process and address their concerns about influence and control. Often, simply seeking their input and addressing their perspective is sufficient to neutralize political opposition.

5. **Don't ignore blockers**: The most dangerous blocker is the one you don't know about. Proactively identify potential blockers and engage them before they become active opponents.

### The Influencer

The influencer is a stakeholder whose opinion carries weight within the committee but who doesn't have direct decision authority. Influencers can be internal (a respected senior engineer, a tenured manager, the CEO's chief of staff) or external (an industry analyst, a consulting firm, a board member).

**Internal Influencer Profiles**:

| Influencer Type | Influence Source | Engagement Approach |
|----------------|-----------------|-------------------|
| **Senior IC** | Technical respect from peers | Provide deep technical content and peer validation |
| **Tenured manager** | Institutional knowledge and relationships | Respect their experience, show how the solution builds on existing practices |
| **EA/Chief of Staff** | Direct access to executive thinking | Provide executive-level materials and strategic context |
| **Finance business partner** | Budget analysis and financial advisory | Provide detailed financial models and ROI documentation |
| **Procurement lead** | Process control and vendor management | Engage early, provide standard documentation, respect the process |

**External Influencer Profiles**:

| Influencer Type | Influence Source | Engagement Approach |
|----------------|-----------------|-------------------|
| **Industry analyst** | Category expertise and vendor evaluations | Brief analysts, participate in research, maintain strong relationships |
| **Consulting firm** | Implementation and strategy advisory | Partner on implementations, provide reference architecture |
| **Board member** | Strategic oversight and advisory | Ensure board-level narrative is clear and compelling |
| **Peer network** | Informal advice and experience sharing | Provide excellent customer experiences that generate positive word-of-mouth |

## Committee Dynamics: Patterns and Anti-Patterns

### Common Committee Patterns

Understanding common committee patterns helps sellers anticipate how the buying process will unfold and prepare appropriate engagement strategies.

**Pattern 1: The Consensus Model**

In the consensus model, all stakeholders must agree before the purchase proceeds. This is the most common pattern in mid-market companies and for cross-functional purchases. Consensus-driven decisions are slower but more durable — once the group agrees, the deal is less likely to be reversed.

*Characteristics*: Multiple stakeholders with equal influence, long evaluation cycles, emphasis on group alignment, frequent internal meetings to discuss progress.

*Seller Strategy*: Focus on multi-threading to ensure all stakeholders are engaged and aligned. Provide materials for each stakeholder's specific concerns. Facilitate group alignment by identifying and addressing the most skeptical stakeholder first.

**Pattern 2: The Hierarchical Model**

In the hierarchical model, a senior executive makes the decision with input from their team. This is common in organizations with strong top-down leadership cultures. The decision is faster but carries higher reversal risk if the executive changes their mind or leaves.

*Characteristics*: Clear decision-maker, team provides input but doesn't have veto power, faster decision cycles, executive engagement is decisive.

*Seller Strategy*: Focus on winning the executive while ensuring the team provides positive input. Provide executive-ready materials for the decision-maker and technical materials for the team. Don't ignore the team — they can influence the executive's decision even if they don't have formal authority.

**Pattern 3: The Committee Vote Model**

In the committee vote model, a formal evaluation committee scores vendors against defined criteria and makes a recommendation. This is common in large enterprises and government organizations. The process is highly structured and often involves RFPs.

*Characteristics*: Formal evaluation criteria, scoring matrix, multiple vendor presentations, structured reference checks, long timelines.

*Seller Strategy*: Understand the evaluation criteria and scoring methodology as early as possible. Tailor presentations to maximize score against published criteria. Provide reference customers that match the committee's priorities. Ensure all documentation is complete and submitted on time.

**Pattern 4: The Champion-Driven Model**

In the champion-driven model, a strong internal champion drives the decision and the committee follows their recommendation. This is the most favorable pattern for sellers because it minimizes the need for broad stakeholder engagement.

*Characteristics*: Single strong champion, other stakeholders defer to the champion's expertise, fast decision cycles, champion has significant organizational influence.

*Seller Strategy*: Invest heavily in champion enablement. Provide the champion with everything they need to sell internally. Monitor champion health closely. Develop backup champions in case the primary champion loses influence.

### Common Committee Anti-Patterns

**Anti-Pattern 1: The Phantom Committee**

The seller believes they are working with the full buying committee, but unknown stakeholders are influencing the decision behind the scenes. This is the most common cause of surprise deal losses.

*Warning Signs*: The champion can't explain why the decision is delayed, new stakeholders appear at late-stage meetings, the champion seems to be getting pushback they can't explain.

*Prevention Strategy*: Proactively map the buying committee using organizational research. Ask the champion directly who else will be involved. Engage Scout Agent to identify additional stakeholders through LinkedIn and email analysis.

**Anti-Pattern 2: The Consensus Trap**

The committee requires unanimous agreement, but one stakeholder consistently raises new objections or moves the goalposts. The deal enters an infinite evaluation loop.

*Warning Signs*: The same stakeholder raises new concerns after previous concerns are addressed, evaluation criteria keep expanding, timeline keeps extending without clear end date.

*Prevention Strategy*: Establish evaluation criteria and success metrics upfront. Create a structured evaluation timeline with defined milestones. Engage the blocking stakeholder directly to understand and address their concerns. If the blocker can't be satisfied, escalate to the economic buyer for a decision.

**Anti-Pattern 3: The Disconnected Executive**

The champion and evaluation team are aligned, but the economic buyer is disengaged and makes a decision based on incomplete information — often rejecting the purchase because they don't understand the value.

*Warning Signs*: The economic buyer hasn't attended any meetings, the champion says "my boss is too busy to get involved", final approval keeps getting delayed.

*Prevention Strategy*: Ensure the economic buyer receives executive-level materials throughout the process, not just at the final approval stage. Offer executive briefings at convenient times. Provide the champion with executive-ready materials they can share asynchronously.

**Anti-Pattern 4: The Competing Internal Priorities**

Multiple stakeholders have conflicting priorities for the same budget, and the purchase gets caught in an internal political battle that has nothing to do with the solution's merits.

*Warning Signs*: Different stakeholders express different value expectations, budget becomes a recurring concern, internal meetings result in no clear decision.

*Prevention Strategy*: Position the solution as serving multiple priorities simultaneously. Help the champion build a coalition of stakeholders who each benefit from the purchase. Provide ROI analysis that addresses each stakeholder's specific priority.

## Mapping the Buying Committee

### The Committee Mapping Framework

Effective committee mapping requires both structural analysis (who holds what role) and relational analysis (how stakeholders relate to each other). The following framework provides a systematic approach:

**Step 1: Identify All Stakeholders**

Use multiple data sources to identify every individual who may influence the purchase:

| Data Source | Information Revealed | LeadReach Agent |
|------------|---------------------|-----------------|
| CRM contacts | Existing relationships and engagement history | Scout enriches with title, role, and org chart data |
| LinkedIn research | Reporting structure, tenure, career history | Scout maps organizational relationships |
| Email thread analysis | Who's included on correspondence about the purchase | Scout identifies participants and their engagement level |
| Meeting attendees | Who shows up to demos, evaluations, and discussions | Scout tracks meeting participant patterns |
| Champion input | Insider knowledge of the decision process | Forge generates discovery questions to surface unknown stakeholders |
| Job postings | Roles the company is hiring for that suggest relevant needs | Scout monitors for relevant job postings at target accounts |
| Press releases | Leadership changes and organizational restructuring | Scout tracks trigger events that affect committee composition |

**Step 2: Assign Committee Roles**

For each identified stakeholder, determine their committee role based on title, engagement pattern, and influence indicators:

| Role | Title Indicators | Engagement Pattern | Influence Markers |
|------|-----------------|-------------------|-------------------|
| Economic buyer | VP, Chief, Head of | Infrequent but decisive | Budget authority, final sign-off |
| Technical buyer | Director of Eng/IT, Architect, Senior Engineer | Detailed questions, architecture focus | Technical veto power |
| Champion | Manager, Senior IC, Director | High engagement, proactive communication | Internal advocacy, stakeholder introductions |
| End user | IC, Team Lead, Coordinator | Use-case questions, workflow focus | Adoption influence, team feedback |
| Blocker | Any title | Objections, delays, competing priorities | Opposition influence, can stall or kill |
| Influencer | Senior IC, EA, Analyst | Opinion-driven engagement | Peer respect, advisory role |
| Procurement | Procurement Manager, Legal | Process-focused, compliance-driven | Process control, contract negotiation |

**Step 3: Map Relationships**

Understanding how stakeholders relate to each other is as important as understanding their individual roles. Key relationship types to map:

- **Reporting relationships**: Who reports to whom (formal authority)
- **Peer relationships**: Who works closely with whom (informal influence)
- **Champion relationships**: Who trusts the champion's recommendation
- **Adversarial relationships**: Who has tension or competing priorities with whom
- **Mentor/mentee relationships**: Who influences whom through advisory relationships

**Step 4: Identify Gaps and Risks**

Compare the mapped committee against the ideal committee for the deal type and identify gaps:

| Gap Type | Risk Level | Action Required |
|----------|-----------|----------------|
| No economic buyer engaged | Critical | Must engage budget-holder before deal can close |
| No technical buyer consulted | High | Technical objections will surface late |
| No end user input | Medium | Adoption risk if end users aren't consulted |
| Unknown stakeholders | High | Phantom committee risk — discover and engage |
| Active blocker unidentified | Critical | Deal may be sabotaged from within |
| No champion identified | Critical | No internal advocate to drive the deal |

## Committee Consensus Building

### The Consensus Challenge

Building consensus across a buying committee is the primary challenge in enterprise B2B sales. Research from Gartner indicates that the typical buying committee spends 27% of the purchase process building internal consensus — more time than any other activity. Sellers who facilitate consensus building close significantly more deals than those who leave the committee to navigate it alone.

The fundamental challenge of consensus building is that different stakeholders evaluate the purchase on different criteria, have different risk tolerances, and have different priorities. A solution that looks perfect to the VP of Sales may look risky to the CISO and expensive to the CFO. The seller's job is to help the committee find common ground — a shared perspective that addresses each stakeholder's concerns while building collective confidence in the decision.

### Consensus Building Strategies

**Strategy 1: The Common Ground Approach**

Identify the one or two outcomes that all stakeholders agree on and build the business case around those shared priorities. Even if stakeholders disagree on specifics, they often agree on the general direction.

*Example*: The VP of Sales wants faster pipeline, the VP of Marketing wants better attribution, and the CFO wants lower cost-per-acquisition. The common ground: "This solution improves revenue efficiency, which benefits pipeline, attribution, and CPA simultaneously."

**Strategy 2: The Sequential Alignment Approach**

Align stakeholders one at a time, starting with the most influential and moving to the most skeptical. Each alignment creates momentum for the next.

*Process*: Start with the champion → align the economic buyer → address the technical buyer → neutralize the blocker → confirm end user support.

**Strategy 3: The Coalition Building Approach**

Build a coalition of stakeholders who collectively support the purchase. The coalition should include representatives from each major committee role to create broad-based support.

*Process*: Identify 3-4 stakeholders who are most receptive → provide each with role-specific value propositions → facilitate connections between them → encourage them to advocate collectively.

**Strategy 4: The Decision Framework Approach**

Provide the committee with a structured decision framework that makes it easy to compare options objectively. This reduces political dynamics and focuses the conversation on criteria rather than opinions.

*Components*: Defined evaluation criteria, weighted scoring matrix, structured reference check process, POC success metrics, and timeline with milestones.

### Consensus Killers and How to Address Them

| Consensus Killer | Description | Prevention | Recovery |
|-----------------|------------|-----------|---------|
| **Scope creep** | Evaluation criteria keep expanding | Define and agree on criteria upfront | Refer back to agreed criteria, reject new requirements |
| **Competing alternatives** | Different stakeholders champion different vendors | Understand each stakeholder's preference early | Focus on differentiators that address the specific stakeholder's concern |
| **Analysis paralysis** | Too much information, no decision | Create structured evaluation timeline | Propose a decision date and create urgency |
| **Stakeholder fatigue** | Committee members lose interest in the process | Keep evaluations focused and efficient | Simplify the process, reduce the number of meetings required |
| **Executive intervention** | Senior leader overrides committee recommendation | Keep executives informed throughout the process | Brief the executive directly with a compelling summary |
| **Budget surprise** | Budget becomes unavailable mid-process | Confirm budget availability early | Help the champion build a stronger business case for reallocation |

## Buying Committee Dynamics by Deal Stage

### Stage 1: Problem Recognition (0-5% of Committee Engaged)

At this stage, one or two stakeholders have identified a problem and are beginning to research solutions. The committee doesn't exist yet — it's just an individual or small group feeling a pain point.

**Seller Objective**: Become part of the problem-solving conversation early. Provide educational content that helps the stakeholder articulate the problem and understand solution approaches.

**LeadReach Agent Role**: Scout identifies the initial engagement signal and alerts the seller. Forge delivers educational content tailored to the stakeholder's role and pain point. Judge scores the signal strength and recommends engagement priority.

### Stage 2: Solution Exploration (5-30% of Committee Engaged)

The initial stakeholders are actively researching solutions and beginning to involve colleagues. The committee is forming but isn't fully defined. New stakeholders are being added as the problem becomes more broadly understood.

**Seller Objective**: Help the forming committee understand the solution landscape. Position your solution as a category leader. Identify and begin enabling the most likely champion.

**LeadReach Agent Role**: Scout monitors expanding engagement (new stakeholders from the same company engaging with content). Forge delivers solution-specific content and offers evaluations. Judge maps the forming committee and identifies gaps.

### Stage 3: Requirements Building (30-50% of Committee Engaged)

The committee is defining requirements and evaluation criteria. This is a critical stage because the criteria that get defined often determine which vendor wins. Influencing requirements early is far more effective than trying to compete against unfavorable criteria later.

**Seller Objective**: Influence evaluation criteria to favor your solution's strengths. Help the committee define requirements that are specific enough to differentiate vendors but not so narrow that they exclude your solution.

**LeadReach Agent Role**: Scout identifies when the committee is in requirements-building mode (RFP activity, multiple stakeholder meetings, evaluation document requests). Forge provides requirements templates and criteria suggestions that align with your solution's strengths. Judge assesses competitive position based on known criteria.

### Stage 4: Vendor Evaluation (50-80% of Committee Engaged)

The full committee is now engaged in evaluating vendors. This typically involves structured presentations, POCs, reference checks, and scoring. Each stakeholder evaluates based on their role-specific criteria.

**Seller Objective**: Maximize your score across all evaluation criteria. Address each stakeholder's specific concerns. Differentiate from competitors on criteria that matter most to the economic buyer.

**LeadReach Agent Role**: Scout tracks stakeholder engagement patterns during evaluation (who attends, what questions they ask, how they respond to presentations). Forge generates stakeholder-specific follow-up materials after each evaluation touchpoint. Judge monitors competitive signals and deal health indicators.

### Stage 5: Decision & Approval (80-100% of Committee Engaged)

The committee has completed evaluation and is moving toward a decision. Final approval often involves stakeholders who weren't involved in the evaluation — the CFO, legal, procurement. These late-stage stakeholders can introduce new requirements or objections.

**Seller Objective**: Facilitate the final decision by ensuring all remaining concerns are addressed. Support the champion through the internal approval process. Navigate procurement and legal efficiently.

**LeadReach Agent Role**: Scout identifies late-stage stakeholders entering the process and alerts the seller. Forge generates procurement-ready documentation (security questionnaires, legal templates, pricing proposals). Judge monitors for stall signals and recommends acceleration strategies.

## Industry-Specific Committee Dynamics

### Technology Companies

Technology companies tend to have technical buyers with strong influence and relatively fast decision processes. The CTO/VP of Engineering often serves as both technical and economic buyer for engineering tools. Consensus requirements are lower because technology leaders have more autonomy.

**Typical Committee**: CTO + VP of Engineering + Engineering Manager + Security Review (4-6 stakeholders)

**Timeline**: 4-8 weeks for department tools, 8-12 weeks for platform purchases

**Key Dynamic**: Technical merit is heavily weighted. The engineer's evaluation often determines the outcome regardless of business case strength.

### Financial Services

Financial services organizations have the most complex buying committees due to regulatory requirements, risk management processes, and multi-layered approval structures. Procurement, compliance, and legal play significant roles that can significantly extend timelines.

**Typical Committee**: VP + Director + IT + Security + Compliance + Legal + Procurement + Risk Management (8-15 stakeholders)

**Timeline**: 12-20 weeks for standard purchases, 20-30+ weeks for regulated technology

**Key Dynamic**: Compliance and risk management have effective veto power. Security review is the most common cause of delays. Proactive security documentation is essential.

### Healthcare

Healthcare organizations have complex committees driven by HIPAA requirements, patient safety concerns, and multi-stakeholder governance structures. Clinical and administrative stakeholders often have different priorities.

**Typical Committee**: VP + Clinical stakeholder + IT + Security + Compliance + Privacy Officer + Procurement (7-12 stakeholders)

**Timeline**: 10-16 weeks for administrative tools, 16-24 weeks for clinical technology

**Key Dynamic**: Privacy and compliance requirements drive committee structure. Clinical stakeholder buy-in is required for any technology that affects patient care workflows.

### Manufacturing

Manufacturing organizations have operations-driven committees with strong influence from plant managers and operations leadership. IT often has less influence than in other industries, and operational impact is the primary evaluation criterion.

**Typical Committee**: VP of Operations + Plant Manager + IT + Supply Chain + Procurement (5-8 stakeholders)

**Timeline**: 8-14 weeks for operations tools, 6-10 weeks for IT-only purchases

**Key Dynamic**: Operational stakeholders have outsized influence. Demonstrations that show real production environments are more effective than theoretical presentations.
